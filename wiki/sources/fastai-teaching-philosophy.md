## Summary

Rachel Thomas lays out the pedagogical philosophy behind fast.ai's deep learning courses. Inspired by Paul Lockhart's *A Mathematician's Lament* and David Perkins' "whole game" method, the course rejects prerequisites-first teaching in favor of getting students building real models immediately, then drilling deeper over time. Good education is inclusive, error-welcoming, example-driven, and evidence-backed.

## Key Points

- "Elementitis" — teaching isolated pieces before letting students see the whole game — is the disease of conventional technical education.
- fast.ai starts with a complete working model on a real problem in the first 30 minutes, then gradually unpacks how the tools work.
- Good education should not require a specific background, expensive hardware, or an elite college.
- Spaced repetition + whole-game learning = revisiting topics repeatedly at increasing depth, always returning to the big picture.
- Traditional education optimizes for passing tests, not for finding/scoping interesting problems — a critical weakness for research careers.
- Mistakes should be welcomed, not stigmatized; interactive notebooks make this culture easy to cultivate.

## Quotes

> "If you truly understand something, you can explain it in an accessible way, and maybe even implement it in Excel!"

> "I was great at problem sets and exams… but I could no longer learn every incremental building block before setting to work on a task."

> "Lockhart argues that it would be better to not teach math at all, then to teach such a mangled form of it that alienates most of the population."

## My Take

This is a manifesto as much as a teaching philosophy. The Lockhart parallel is apt — deep learning has its own gatekeepers who demand you derive loss functions before touching code. The "whole game" framework resonates deeply: it's the same tension between map and territory that shows up everywhere in learning. The self-criticism at the end — that Rachel herself was trained to solve given problems, not find them — is honest and useful.
