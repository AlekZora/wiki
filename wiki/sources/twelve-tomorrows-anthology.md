## Summary

An anthology of near-future science fiction stories commissioned by MIT Technology Review, paired with an extended interview with Neal Stephenson about the role of science fiction in shaping technological culture. The stories imagine near-future applications of technologies currently covered by the magazine—biofuels, genetic engineering, communications, space. The Stephenson interview is the intellectual backbone: he argues SF writers have a moral responsibility to produce optimistic, actionable visions of the future.

## Key Ideas

- **SF as prototyping**: Science fiction's utility isn't prediction—it's providing "a coherent picture of an alternate reality in which some innovation happened," including the social and economic context. It's a magnetic field that lines up iron filings.
- **The optimism imperative**: Stephenson argues that dystopian SF is a self-fulfilling prophecy—if every depiction of the future is grim, there's no incentive to build a better one. Not a call for naivete but for actionable hope.
- **Project Hieroglyph**: Stephenson's response to a challenge from Arizona State president Michael Crow, who accused SF writers of "slacking off" by producing only dystopia. Hieroglyph was an experiment in commissioning optimistic near-future SF from top writers.
- **Innovation stagnation**: Stephenson's diagnosis—the West got distracted by the Internet for a generation and stopped doing hard physical things (space, energy, infrastructure). He was working on fixing this.
- **The internet pause**: "What I'm hoping is that when we look back on this era 100 years from now, we'll say... the Internet happened and everything got put on hold for a generation while we absorbed it. Then we got going again."
- **Sword holding**: Stephenson's digression on how knowing how to properly hold a sword changes your relationship to history and embodied knowledge. Practical physical competence as epistemological anchor.

## Quotes

> "If every depiction of the future is grim and shows a world that's been destroyed... it doesn't create much of an incentive to work towards building the future."

> "Science fiction seems to have played a role in partly inspiring people to go into [technical fields] and, to some degree, shaping what they did once they got into a position to do something."

## My Take

The Stephenson interview alone is worth the book. The SF-as-prototyping frame is more sophisticated than the usual "SF predicts the future" narrative—it's about creating shared mental models that coordinate effort. Project Hieroglyph is an interesting model for AI: instead of asking what AI will do, ask SF writers to write stories in which AI-assisted [specific thing] went well, with enough detail that an engineer could use it as a design brief. The innovation stagnation argument connects to Hamming's "important problems" framework: if SF writers aren't asking what the important problems are, who is?
