## Summary

Danielle Riendeau reflects on GDC 2026, where she attended Adam Saltsman's (Finji) talk about CorgiSpace — a year-long project of making tiny games with short development cycles. Saltsman's manifesto: separate ideas from formulas, work with the grain of your tools, make decisions that are "easy but not obvious," and keep the practice generative rather than draining. The talk gave Riendeau (and many others weathering the industry contraction) renewed hope in hobbyist game development.

## Key Points

- The game industry in 2026 is in a painful contraction: mass layoffs, studio closures, seasoned veterans turning to retail or dishwashing between job interviews.
- Adam Saltsman's CorgiSpace: a collection of small games made over a year, each with short legs (Corgi = short-legged) — deliberately scoped to fit in nooks of a busy schedule.
- The manifesto:
  - **Idea vs. formula**: an idea is the hot glowing heart of the game; a formula is an imprint of that idea that tends to calcify. "Ideas are almost always good — formulas are almost always bad."
  - **Work with the grain**: find the natural flow of a tool or process and let it guide you toward gameplay that is "easy, but not obvious."
  - **Notice things**: simple discovery is crucial for both players and developers.
- This practice is *rejuvenating* — "it gives more energy than it takes."
- Derek Yu's advice: scope small to build the habit of finishing games.
- Riendeau's tension: she wants to make tiny games, but also wants them to mean something, not just be "fun garbage."

## Quotes

> "In general, ideas, if you actually start working on them, are almost always good—and formulas are almost always bad."

> "I've been using this approach to tuck these little games into the nooks and crannies of my schedule for over a year now, and instead of being exhausting or burdensome, it's been a relief."

> "These legs may be short, but for the first time in a long, long while, I feel like they may have some strength again."

## My Take

The idea/formula distinction is one of the better ways I've seen articulated why so many games (and creative projects generally) feel derivative even when well-executed — they're implementing a formula in the image of an idea, not the idea itself. The "easy but not obvious" framing pairs nicely with the Door Problem: both are about finding the design that works so well it disappears. The piece is also valuable as a document of the industry's emotional state in early 2026.
