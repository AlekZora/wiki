## Summary

A YC partner (Jared) walks through a framework for finding and evaluating startup ideas, drawing on analysis of the top 100 YC companies by valuation, Paul Graham's essays, and patterns observed from thousands of rejected applications. The talk covers the most common founder mistakes, a 10-question framework for evaluating any idea, and seven concrete recipes for generating ideas.

## Key Ideas

### Four common mistakes

1. **SISP (Solution in Search of a Problem)** — Starting with a technology ("what can I apply AI to?") and hunting for a problem. The problem you find will be superficially plausible but not real. Instead, fall in love with a problem first.

2. **Tar pit ideas** — A class of ideas that seem easy and widely applicable but have a hidden structural reason why they can't be solved. Example: an app to coordinate meeting up with friends. Thousands of founders have tried for 20+ years; no one has cracked it. If you want to pursue one anyway: Google it, find prior attempts, talk to those founders, and figure out the hard part.

3. **Not evaluating the idea at all** — Jumping into the first idea without any scrutiny.

4. **Waiting for the perfect idea** — The perfect idea doesn't exist. Think of your initial idea as a good starting point that will morph. Bias toward starting over waiting.

### 10 questions to evaluate a startup idea

1. **Founder-market fit** — Are you the right team for this? This is arguably the most important criterion. Reframe your search as: find a good idea *for your team*, not a good idea in the abstract.
2. **How big is the market?** — Needs to be either large now (billion-dollar+) or small but rapidly growing (e.g., Coinbase when crypto was tiny).
3. **How acute is the problem?** — Brex example: before Brex, YC startups literally could not get a corporate credit card. When the alternative is *nothing*, that's an acute problem.
4. **Do you have competition?** — Counter-intuitively, competition is a good sign. It means a real market exists. No competition often means no one wants the product. You just need a new insight vs. entrenched incumbents.
5. **Do you personally want this? Do you know people who want it?** — If both answers are no, that's a serious warning sign.
6. **Has something recently changed that made this possible or necessary?** — New technology, regulation, or behavior shift. Example: Checkr (background checks via API) emerged because gig economy companies suddenly needed to run checks at scale on huge contractor pools.
7. **Is there a proxy?** — A large company doing something similar that validates demand. Example: Rappi (food delivery in Latin America) used DoorDash as its proxy.
8. **Is this something you'd want to work on for years?** — Tricky. Many great ideas are in boring spaces (payroll, tax accounting). Passion often develops as the company starts working, not before.
9. **Is this a scalable business?** — Pure software: yes, check it off. Services/agencies involving high-skill human labor: dangerous, not scalable.
10. **Is this a good idea space?** — Idea spaces have wildly different hit rates. Fintech infrastructure and vertical SaaS for enterprise had astonishingly high hit rates over the last decade. Consumer hardware and ad tech did not. Pick a fertile space — if your first idea in it doesn't work, there are adjacent ideas to drift into.

### Three things that seem like weaknesses but are strengths

- **Hard to get started** — Schlep blindness (Paul Graham's term). Stripe required getting a bank deal and learning credit card infrastructure minutiae — that scared everyone away, leaving a $100B opportunity on the table.
- **In a boring space** — Gusto (payroll software). Boring ideas have higher hit rates because most founders ignore them. The day-to-day of any startup is the same regardless of how "fun" the idea sounds.
- **Existing competitors** — Dropbox entered as the 20th cloud storage company. Most people didn't use any of the 19 others — strong signal that the problem was real but unsolved. Drew's specific insight: integrate into the OS to auto-sync instead of manual upload.

### How to come up with startup ideas

**Long game (organic ideas):**
- 70%+ of YC top 100 got their idea organically, not by sitting down to brainstorm.
- Become an expert in something valuable (work at a startup in a domain).
- Build things you find interesting even if they don't seem like businesses (Replit origin story).

**Seven recipes (ordered by likelihood of leading to a good idea):**

1. **Start with your team's expertise** — Ideas generated this way have automatic founder-market fit. Example: Rezi (OpenDoor for rentals) — founders were real estate + debt financing experts who searched only within real estate × fintech.
2. **Problem you've personally encountered, ideally in an unusual position to see** — VetCove (Amazon for veterinary supplies): founders' dad was a vet; they noticed archaic 1-800 ordering. Thousands of vets knew the problem but don't start tech companies; thousands of devs didn't know the problem existed.
3. **Things you personally wish existed** — DoorDash example. Warning: this recipe is most likely to lead to tar pit ideas; ask why the thing doesn't exist yet before building.
4. **Things that have recently changed** — Gather.town pivoted to virtual hangout space at pandemic onset.
5. **Variants on successful recent companies** — Nuvo Cargo = Flexport for Latin America. Founder picked it analytically: large market, good proxy, personal operational strengths, relevant connections.
6. **Talk to people in a fertile idea space** — Pick a space, go talk to potential customers *and* founders. A to B (fuel cards for truckers) did this by literally driving to truck stops and talking to drivers. They iterated through many ideas before fuel cards.
7. **Big broken industries** — Any large industry that seems obviously dysfunctional is probably ripe for disruption.

**Bonus:** Find a co-founder on platforms like Startup School who already has an idea — you get co-founder + idea at once.

### Closing

It's often hard to know if an idea is good before launching. If you've done the work and are still on the fence: just launch it.

## My Take

Dense, practical talk — basically a checklist dressed as a narrative. The founder-market fit criterion is the sleeper insight: it reframes idea search from "what's a good idea" to "what's a good idea for *us*," which is a meaningfully different question. The tar pit concept is useful as a sanity filter. The A to B case study (truck stop interviews) is the most actionable example of systematically acquiring domain expertise and using it to find an idea from scratch.
