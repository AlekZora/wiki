## Summary

Rachel Thomas argues that the popular belief that boredom is beneficial for children is contradicted by research showing boredom (or "boreout") is harmful for adults. She draws a sharp distinction between boredom and unstructured free play, and points out that the same educational systems praising boredom for kids are often simply under-stimulating children, not giving them healthy free time. The article pushes back on false binaries (screens vs. play, busy vs. bored) and advocates for appropriate intellectual challenge.

## Key Points

- "Boreout" — chronic boredom and understimulation — is documented as harmful in the workplace (causes stress, disengagement, and quitting). Yet boredom is praised as healthy for children.
- Boredom ≠ unstructured free time. Real free play is self-directed, interesting, and generative. Boredom is dullness and disengagement.
- Her daughter became passive, curious-less, and upset by mistakes (at piano, chess) while attending school — a result of chronic under-stimulation. Homeschooling restored her curiosity quickly.
- Keeping children busy does not cure boredom — the problem is the quality of engagement, not the quantity.
- Erik Hoel's argument: young children have an "endless appetite for new information"; delaying reading until age 5-6 deprives them of a tool to satisfy it.
- False binary: children can both have free time AND engage in intellectual challenges; overloaded schedules and under-stimulation are both problems, not opposites.
- There are skills that "open up worlds" — reading, coding, touch-typing. Children benefit from learning these early.

## Quotes

## My Take

The adult/child double standard Rachel identifies is real and worth naming. The distinction between boredom and free play is precise and useful. I find the early reading argument compelling — there's a strong parallel to the fast.ai teaching philosophy: don't withhold tools from people who are ready to use them. The "skills that open up worlds" framing is memorable.
