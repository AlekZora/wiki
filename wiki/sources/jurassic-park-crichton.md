## Summary

InGen, a biotech company, uses ancient DNA preserved in amber to resurrect dinosaurs on a remote Costa Rican island as a theme park. When a systems failure cascades through the park's interconnected infrastructure, the park's mathematician consultant Ian Malcolm has been predicting from the beginning that this outcome was inevitable. A thriller wrapped around a serious argument about the epistemological limits of complex systems and the ethical failures of commercialized science.

## Key Ideas

- **Chaos theory as narrative spine**: Malcolm is a chaos theorist who argues that any system as complex as a functioning ecosystem cannot be controlled—only managed until it fails. Small perturbations amplify unpredictably. The park was doomed before it opened.
- **Biotechnology hubris**: Crichton's prologue (historical context) establishes the book as a warning about the then-nascent biotech boom: unlike other tech revolutions, biotech happens in thousands of uncoordinated labs, is self-replicating by nature, and has no global oversight mechanism.
- **The iteration problem**: Malcolm points out that life evolves—the dinosaurs aren't static exhibits. They adapt and find ways around containment. You can't build a cage for something that hasn't existed for 65 million years.
- **Science captured by commerce**: By the early 1990s, nearly every molecular biologist in the US had a financial interest in biotech companies. Crichton identified this capture as the core danger: science's independence—the thing that makes it self-correcting—was being sold.
- **Complexity vs. complicatedness**: A complicated system (lots of parts) can be understood; a complex system (lots of interactions with feedback loops and emergent behavior) cannot. The park was complex; its builders thought it was merely complicated.
- **The nedry failure**: A disgruntled underpaid employee shuts down the park's systems for side money. The single human point of failure in an otherwise automated system. Relevant for any automated high-stakes system.

## My Take

Jurassic Park is one of the few thrillers that is actually an argument. The Malcolm effect (chaos in complex systems) is directly applicable to AI deployment: we build systems with millions of interacting components, then assume they'll behave as the sum of their parts. They don't. The biotech-capture-by-commerce point has only intensified—it now applies equally to AI research. The most actionable insight: the thing that kills you is not the most dangerous component, it's the second-order interaction you didn't model. For product building: never underestimate the Nedry problem—the insider who has just enough access to cause a catastrophic failure.
