## Summary

Edmond Dantès, a young sailor on the cusp of happiness, is falsely imprisoned in the Château d'If through the jealousy of rivals. After years of captivity, he escapes with the help of the Abbé Faria, discovers a vast treasure on the island of Monte Cristo, and reinvents himself as the Count of Monte Cristo — wealthy, cosmopolitan, and dedicated to exacting perfect vengeance on those who destroyed his life. The novel traces his methodical dismantling of his enemies' families and fortunes, while questioning the moral cost of revenge.

## Key Ideas

- Dantès's transformation is the core: from innocent to god-like avenger. He loses his humanity in the process and must decide whether revenge is worth the self he has become.
- The Abbé Faria is one of literature's great mentors — an imprisoned polymath who educates Dantès for years and leaves him everything.
- Dumas uses the novel to survey all levels of French society — aristocracy, bourgeoisie, criminal underworld, banking, politics, journalism — as systems Dantès learns to manipulate.
- The long game: Dantès waits years between stages of his revenge, carefully positioning each piece before striking. A study in patience and strategic thinking.
- The ending complicates the revenge fantasy — Monte Cristo acknowledges he may have overstepped into God's domain and retreats from the role of instrument of divine justice.

## Quotes

> "All human wisdom is summed up in these two words: Wait and Hope."

> "There is neither happiness nor misery in the world; there is only the comparison of one state with another, nothing more."

## My Take

The novel works as an extended meditation on whether perfect justice is achievable — and whether the person who achieves it can survive the process intact. Dantès's final withdrawal suggests Dumas's answer is no.
