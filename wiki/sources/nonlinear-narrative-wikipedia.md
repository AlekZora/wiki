## Summary

An overview of nonlinear narrative across literature, film, and television. Traces the technique from ancient epics (Homer, Mahabharata, Arabian Nights) through modernist literature (Conrad, Woolf, Faulkner) to contemporary film (Nolan, Tarantino, Lynch) and TV (Lost, Breaking Bad, True Detective). Catalogs the many forms: flashback, reverse chronology, parallel timelines, fragmented structure.

## Key Points

- Ancient roots: in medias res in Homer's Iliad (8th century BC), flashback in Mahabharata
- Modernist explosion: Conrad, Woolf, Ford, Proust, Faulkner abandoned linear order
- Key nonlinear novels: Tristram Shandy, Sound and the Fury, Catch-22, Slaughterhouse-Five, Cloud Atlas
- Film pioneers: Citizen Kane, Rashomon established non-chronological flashback
- 1990s boom: Tarantino's Pulp Fiction popularized nonlinear structure ("Tarantino effect")
- Nolan's full commitment: Following, Memento, Inception, Dunkirk, Oppenheimer
- TV uses: narrative hook (Breaking Bad), mimicking memory (True Detective), exploring past throughout series (Lost, Arrow)
- Godard: "A film should have a beginning, a middle and an end -- but not necessarily in that order"
- Lynch uses nonlinear + surrealism to dissolve causality entirely

## My Take

The taxonomy of TV approaches is most useful: nonlinear as hook (show endpoint first), as memory mimicry (unreliable recall), as parallel timeline exploration. For a daily episodic format, the "certain episodes explore the past" model (Penny Dreadful) or "future/past throughout" (Lost) could work. Breaking Bad's cold open technique is especially effective for short-form episodes.

## Project Connection

Essential reference for the sci-fi series structure. The show could use nonlinear narrative to weaponize information asymmetry -- showing audiences fragments from different timelines that reveal hidden agendas before characters become aware. The True Detective model (unreliable memory under interrogation) maps perfectly to a paranoid setting where characters are being debriefed about events they may not truthfully recall.
