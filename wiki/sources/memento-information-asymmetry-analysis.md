## Summary

A close analysis of how information asymmetry isn't merely a structural device in *Memento* but is the film's philosophical argument. The reverse chronology forces the audience to inhabit Leonard's epistemically fragmented state rather than observe it, making the film's themes about memory, identity, and self-deception experiential rather than expository.

## Key Points

- **Dual-timeline architecture**: color sequences move backward (simulating Leonard's inability to form new memories); black-and-white sequences move forward chronologically. The audience always knows the result of an event before knowing its cause. The question is always "why did *that* already happen?" not "what happens next?"
- **Memory as an unreliable information system**: the film's deepest argument is that memory itself is a form of information asymmetry. Leonard externalizes cognition (tattoos, Polaroids, notes) believing them objective — but the reverse chronology exposes how every note was created by a subjective human in a manipulable moment. The audience experiences this same unreliability firsthand.
- **The ultimate asymmetric reveal**: the convergence of both timelines reveals Leonard has been *manipulating himself*. Because color sequences withheld causal information scene by scene, this revelation doesn't arrive as a twist — it retroactively reframes every prior scene. Nolan's plot points here are *informational*, not actional.
- **Identity without memory**: by sharing Leonard's informational deprivation, the audience inhabits rather than observes his fractured self. Three philosophical questions the asymmetry raises:
  1. Who are we without memory?
  2. Can morality exist without context? (Leonard can't retain consequences; the audience judges without the moral framework chronology normally provides)
  3. How much of reality is self-constructed? Coherent identity requires narrative; narrative requires selective, manipulated information.
- **Curiosity over suspense**: the film abandons outcome questions ("will Leonard succeed?") entirely in favor of information questions ("why did X happen? who is lying?"). This only works because the information asymmetry is total — viewer and Leonard are equally stranded.
- The color sequences shift from subjective to objective as the film progresses, gradually closing the information gap — but only at the moment of maximum thematic impact.

## My Take

The formulation "a self-sealing system that enables action and identity continuity while locking in error" is the most precise description of what Leonard's condition does. The film argues that this is just how all human cognition works — we achieve certainty without verification. The information asymmetry isn't a stylistic choice; it's the only structure that could make that argument experientially rather than didactically.
