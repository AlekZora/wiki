*Frontiers in Psychology* 12:565202, doi 10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202.

## Summary

A meta-analysis of **MCII**: picture the desired future, name the obstacle in present reality,
then form an "if obstacle, then I will…" plan. Across 21 articles, 24 effect sizes and 15,907
participants, MCII improved goal attainment with a small-to-medium effect (g = 0.336). Only one
of six tested moderators mattered: **intervention type**. Face-to-face sessions with an
experimenter (g = 0.465) beat document-based ones done alone, online, at home or in class
(g = 0.277). There are signs of publication bias, and the trim-and-fill correction lowers the
effect to g = 0.242. A single MOOC study supplied 87% of all participants and was excluded from
the moderator analysis, which makes those results fragile.

## Key Points

- **Main effect:** g = 0.336 (95% CI 0.229–0.443); after trim-and-fill correction, **g = 0.242**. Medium heterogeneity (I² ≈ 59%).
- **"Interactive" means a human:** the stronger arm was an *experimenter* intervention, a meeting between an experimenter and one participant or a small group, usually in a lab, hospital or rehab centre. The weaker arm was a "document intervention". The authors suggest two reasons: the interpersonal relationship, and participants writing **low-quality plans** when left alone with a document.
- **No difference** by publication status, age, goal domain (academic, health, relationship, personal) or how outcomes were measured. Expectation of success couldn't be tested because nearly everyone had high expectations, since studies told people to pick feasible goals.
- **Plans without reinforcement may not work:** citing Da Silva et al. (2018), implementation-intention interventions *without* reinforcement had no significant effect on adult physical activity; with reinforcement, small-to-medium.
- **Flexibility matters:** rigid plans can make people miss other cues. The authors suggest a meta-plan: "if one MCII strategy fails, I'll form a new MCII strategy."
- **Culture:** a MOOC study found MCII helped learners in individualist cultures but not collectivist ones. The authors suspect a mismatch between individual wishes and collective obstacles.
- Mental contrasting can lead people with **low** expectations of success to let go of a wish. The authors present that as a feature of the method.

## Quotes

> "Studies with interventions based on interactions between participants and experimenters (g = 0.465) had stronger effects than studies with interventions based on interactions between participants and documents (g = 0.277)."

> "Participants may formulate low-quality MCII strategies in a free document-writing environment … and may have low commitment to the MCII strategy, which will reduce its effectiveness."

## My Take

This corrects the [research report](evidence-based-goal-achievement-system.md), which turned "experimenter > document" into "conversational > static form". The paper's interactive arm was **a person in the room**, not software. Whether a chat with an AI behaves like the experimenter arm or like the document arm is **untested**. The paper's own explanations split the question. The "plan quality" reason is something an AI could plausibly supply, by catching vague obstacles and non-actionable responses. The "interpersonal relationship" reason is the part an AI may not reproduce, and pretending it does is the risk.

**AI lens:** this is a clean, testable product question. Randomize document-style MCII against AI-guided MCII and measure plan quality and follow-through. It's exactly the kind of single-mechanism test the report proposes. And the corrected effect (g ≈ 0.24) is a reminder that the ceiling for any coaching layer is modest.
