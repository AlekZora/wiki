The online summary of RevenueCat's annual report, built on its own customer data: over 115,000
apps and more than $16B in revenue. The full report is a 330+ page PDF behind an email form and
was not read. Exact publication date isn't on the page. It is the 2026 edition, which
TechCrunch covered on 2026-03-10.

## Summary

Benchmarks for subscription apps: conversion, pricing, retention, churn and reactivation, with a
dedicated AI vs. non-AI section. The headline is concentration. The top 10% of apps grew 306%
while the median grew 5.3%, and only 4.6% of new apps reach $10K monthly revenue within two
years. New subscription-app launches grew about 7× since 2022 as AI lowered the cost of
building, so **distribution, not features, is the main barrier**. The finding most relevant to an
AI product: **AI apps sell better but keep subscribers worse.** Trial-to-paid is higher at the
median (8.5% vs 5.6%), and revenue per payer is 41% higher over year one. But 12-month retention
is lower on every plan length (annual 21.1% vs 30.7%; monthly 6.1% vs 9.5%), and refunds are
higher.

## Key Points

- **Day zero decides:** 80–89% of trial starts happen on the day of download, and "the majority of trial cancellations happen on day zero". Users who don't see value immediately rarely come back.
- **Hard paywall vs. freemium:** about 5× higher download-to-paid by day 35 (10.7% vs 2.1%), with nearly identical year-one retention (27% vs 28%). The founder adds that freemium "remains the right call when free users drive word of mouth, network effects, or long-term brand scale."
- **Trials:** longer trials convert better (≤4 days 25.5%, 17–32 days 42.5% median), yet the industry is moving to shorter trials.
- **Price anchors:** $9.99 monthly is the structural anchor. Yearly concentrates at $29.99–$39.99, and Health & Fitness sits around $39.99 a year / $9.99 a month. Higher price tiers convert *better* at the median.
- **Retention:** year-one median retention is 28% annual (down from 31%), 8% monthly, ~1% weekly. The first renewal is the critical inflection. Access model barely affects retention; plan duration does.
- **Why people cancel** (Google Play survey): cost 25–45% and **"not enough usage" 26–40%** across categories. Technical issues only 3–7%.
- **Reactivation:** monthly plans 18–24%, annual 4–6%. Guest essay (Dan Layfield): "users reactivate when the problem comes back, not when your win-back email lands." He also recommends letting users **pause instead of cancel**.
- **AI adoption:** 27.1% of apps are AI-powered. Productivity is 41.1% AI.
- **Category caveat:** this report's "Productivity" bucket is Graphics & Design, Art & Design and Developer tools, *not* goal, habit or planning apps. Don't read Goal Map benchmarks from it.

## My Take

Vendor data with selection bias: RevenueCat customers only, and summary statistics without
methods detail in the online version. It's still the best pricing benchmark the vault now has.
It replaces the single third-party retention figure the vault had before.

Two findings matter for any goal app that wants to charge. First, **"not enough usage" is a top
churn reason**, while good goal-app design argues that a working product may be opened *less*.
Usage-based value perception and progress-based value are in direct conflict. Second, the
reactivation essay describes goals almost exactly: the need comes back when there's a new goal.
That argues for pricing around *goals* rather than continuous access, and for pause instead of
cancel.

**AI lens:** "sells but doesn't stick" is the market-level version of the novelty-vs-craft
problem in [compulsion-vs-craft](../concepts/compulsion-vs-craft.md). AI features create an
early "wow" that converts, but don't by themselves create a reason to return. The report's own
framing, that apps which solve AI retention "early will own their category", treats retention as
the open problem. The vault's answer is that retention has to come from the thing the AI serves
(here, reaching a goal), not from the AI being impressive.
