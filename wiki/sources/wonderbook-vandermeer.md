## Summary

VanderMeer's illustrated guide to writing imaginative fiction—fantasy, science fiction, horror, and the weird. Unlike conventional writing guides that offer rules and formulas, Wonderbook uses visual metaphors, maps, diagrams, and illustrations to convey how imaginative fiction works. Contributors include George R.R. Martin, Neil Gaiman, Ursula K. Le Guin, and others. The book treats story as a living ecosystem rather than a formula, and argues that revision is actually discovery—you find what the story is by writing it, not before.

## Key Ideas

- **Story as ecosystem**: Stories are not mechanisms with inputs and outputs but living systems with interdependent parts. Change one element and the whole ecology shifts. This framing resists the screenwriting-formula approach (three-act structure as universal template).
- **Worldbuilding as ecology not decoration**: Worlds in fiction work when they have their own internal logic that the author has thought through deeply—even if only a fraction of it appears on the page. The iceberg principle applies: readers feel the submerged mass even if they don't see it.
- **The weird as narrative mode**: VanderMeer's specialty is "weird fiction"—stories where the strange is not explained or contained but left as genuine alterity. The weird resists domestication. It produces unease that can't be resolved by the narrative.
- **Revision as discovery**: First drafts are not bad versions of the final story—they're the author figuring out what the story is. The real story emerges through rewriting. This is not linear; it loops.
- **The failure modes of imagination**: VanderMeer catalogs common failures: over-explaining (killing the sense of strangeness), under-developing (the world feels like set decoration), genre defaulting (reaching for familiar elements instead of earned ones).
- **Visual thinking for narrative**: The book's diagrams and illustrations aren't just decorative—they model non-linear, spatial thinking about story structure. Some stories are better understood as maps than as sequences.
- **Character as embodied psychology**: Characters who feel real have specific ways of moving, specific sensory responses, specific cognitive habits. Not just motivations but textures.

## My Take

Wonderbook is the most interesting writing guide I've encountered because it treats fiction as a living system rather than a craft product. The ecosystem metaphor is directly applicable to game design: a game world that has internal ecological logic (economic, social, biological) feels real; one that has surface decoration doesn't. For AI worldbuilding: generative systems that treat world elements as ecologically interdependent would produce more coherent fiction than systems that generate elements independently. The "weird" as a category deserves more attention in AI-generated content—most generative systems regress toward the expected; the weird deliberately stays in the genuinely strange.
