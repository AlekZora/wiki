## Summary

The Wikipedia overview of creativity as a scholarly and psychological subject. Covers the concept's historical evolution (from a purely divine act in antiquity, through Renaissance humanism, to modern psychological study), the major classification frameworks (Four C model, Four P's, Five A's), theories of the creative process (incubation, divergent thinking, conceptual blending, honing theory), psychometric approaches to measuring creativity, and its relationship to intelligence. A broad reference article, not a piece of original argument.

## Key Points

- Consensus definition: production of something both **novel and useful**. Over 100 distinct definitions exist in the literature — the field is far from settled on what creativity even is.
- Ancient cultures had no concept of creativity; they saw art as discovery or imitation (Plato: the painter merely imitates). The modern concept emerged during the Renaissance, when "great men" were credited with originating rather than merely discovering.
- The term "creativity" as applied to human action (not divine creation) was only coined by Alfred North Whitehead in 1927; formal psychological study began with Guilford's 1950 APA address.
- **Four C model** (Kaufman & Beghetto): mini-c (personal insight), little-c (everyday creative expression), Pro-C (professional/vocational), Big-C (historically significant). Useful for calibrating claims about creativity across contexts.
- **Four P's**: Process, Product, Person, Press/Place — the dominant organizing framework for creativity research.
- **Wallas's five-stage model** (1926): preparation → incubation → intimation → illumination → verification. Incubation (unconscious processing away from the problem) is still empirically supported as useful for breaking fixation.
- **Divergent thinking** (generating many possible answers to an open prompt) is often conflated with creativity but is distinct — creative thinking also requires evaluation and selection.
- **Conceptual blending** (Koestler's "bisociation"): creativity as the intersection of two normally separate frames of reference. Underlies humor, analogy, and scientific metaphor.
- **Honing theory** (Gabora): creativity arises from the self-organizing, self-mending nature of a worldview. A creative task forces interaction between the task conception and one's worldview until dissonance is resolved — explains why different works by the same creator share a recognizable voice.
- **Schmidhuber's computational creativity**: creativity, curiosity, and interest are by-products of a system optimizing for learning progress (compression improvement = "wow-effect"). Formally explains why random noise and already-known patterns are both boring; novel regularities are intrinsically interesting.
- Creativity and intelligence are related but distinct. Threshold theory: creativity and IQ are positively correlated up to ~120 IQ; above that, the relationship weakens or disappears. High intelligence is necessary but not sufficient.
- Openness to experience is the personality trait most consistently correlated with creativity across domains.
- Cultural variation: Westerners tend to locate creativity in the individual; Chinese research locates it more in social contribution. 27 of 28 African languages surveyed had no direct translation for "creativity."

## My Take

A useful reference for the definitional landscape. The Schmidhuber framing is the most interesting for AI contexts — it gives a formal account of why novel-but-regular patterns are intrinsically motivating, which maps directly onto questions about what makes AI outputs feel creative or not. The honing theory is also underappreciated: the "voice" consistency across creative works being a signature of a stable worldview is a claim worth thinking about when evaluating whether AI systems have anything like a worldview.
