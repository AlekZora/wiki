## Summary

Comprehensive Wikipedia article covering time travel as a concept across physics, philosophy, and fiction. Forward time travel (time dilation via speed or gravity) is an experimentally confirmed real phenomenon. Backward time travel has theoretical support in general relativity (wormholes, rotating black holes, Tipler cylinders) but faces severe causality objections and Hawking's chronology protection conjecture. Philosophically, the concept forces questions about presentism vs. eternalism, the grandfather paradox, and ontological paradoxes. In fiction, three narrative categories exist: immutable timelines, mutable timelines, and alternate histories.

## Key Points

- Forward time travel is real and observed (GPS satellite clock correction, muon decay); it's a consequence of special and general relativity
- Backward time travel: theoretically allowed by closed timelike curves in GR solutions, but quantum gravity arguments (Hawking) suggest these may be forbidden in practice
- Grandfather paradox: killing your ancestor creates a contradiction — resolved by either Novikov self-consistency (you can't change what happened) or many-worlds (you arrive in a parallel universe)
- Many-worlds interpretation provides paradox-free time travel but means the traveler never returns to their own history
- Fiction taxonomy: immutable timeline (fixed past, no changes possible), mutable timeline (changes allowed, ripple effects), alternate histories (branching parallel worlds)
- Historical progression: from supernatural/sleep-based time displacement (HG Wells 1895 invented the machine metaphor) to relativistic physics-based models
- Stephen Hawking's chronology protection conjecture: the laws of physics conspire to prevent closed timelike curves

## My Take

The many-worlds interpretation of time travel is the most narratively interesting: every time-traveler is already in a foreign timeline, and their home timeline is permanently unreachable. This produces a specific kind of paranoia — you can never go back, you can only go forward in an alien version of history. The Novikov self-consistency principle (you can visit but not change) creates dramatic irony: characters know what will happen but can't prevent it.

## Project Connection

Time travel mechanics are directly relevant to the active sci-fi project's narrative structure. Even without literal time travel, the concepts of immutable vs. mutable timelines, information asymmetry across time, and the impossibility of returning to a prior state map onto character psychology in a confined setting. Characters who know something others don't are living in a different "timeline" of understanding. The chronology protection conjecture — the universe resisting paradox — is an interesting metaphor for institutional cover-ups.
