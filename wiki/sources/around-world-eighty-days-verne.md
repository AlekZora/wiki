## Summary

Phileas Fogg, an eccentric and methodical English gentleman, bets his fortune that he can circumnavigate the globe in eighty days using the newly completed Suez Canal and American transcontinental railroad. He departs London with his French valet Passepartout, pursued by Detective Fix who suspects him of a bank robbery. The novel is as much about character — the rigidity of Fogg, the chaos of Passepartout, the absurdity of Fix's obsession — as it is about geography and pace. The resolution hinges on an overlooked time-zone paradox: traveling east gains a day.

## Key Ideas

- The novel celebrates the shrinking world brought by 19th-century transportation infrastructure — steamships, railroads, telegraphs.
- Fogg is portrayed as almost inhuman in his precision and calm — he functions as a machine at a time when machines were transforming society.
- The time-zone twist is both plot device and philosophical comment: time is relative to observation, not absolute. Fogg wins because he failed to account for his own perspective.
- Despite visiting a dozen countries, Verne's characters rarely engage deeply with local cultures — a critique some make of the novel's imperial-era worldview.

## Quotes

## My Take

The time-zone resolution is more elegant than it gets credit for — it's a consequence of Fogg's own methodology (precise time-keeping) failing to account for its own frame of reference. A lesson about systematic blind spots embedded in a crowd-pleasing adventure.
