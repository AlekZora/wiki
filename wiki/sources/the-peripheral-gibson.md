## Summary

Two timelines, seventy years apart, communicate via a server in a third timeline (China, unclear when). In the near-future South, Flynne and her veteran brother Burton play VR security games to earn money. When Burton lends Flynne his shift in what she thinks is a new game, she witnesses a murder in what turns out to be a real London eighty years in her future. The "future" is post-Jackpot—a slow, multidecade catastrophe that killed about 80% of humanity. The novel explores what happens when two timelines interact, and who profits.

## Key Ideas

- **Stub timelines**: Once the future communicates with the past, a branch forms—a "stub"—that diverges from the original future's history. You can't go back and fix your own past, only branch it. An elegant resolution of grandfather paradoxes.
- **The Jackpot**: Not a single apocalyptic event but a slow convergence of climate change, pandemic, economic collapse, extinction events, and technological disruption that killed 80% of humanity over decades. The survivors don't call it a catastrophe—they call it the Jackpot, because in the chaos, extreme wealth concentrated even further. Gibson's prediction: civilizational collapse is gradual, and the wealthy survive it comfortably.
- **Peripherals**: Remote-controlled bodies in the future that past-people can inhabit. Flynne's peripheral is a customized human body she controls from her timeline. Raises questions about identity, embodiment, and what "being there" means.
- **The haptics**: Burton's combat-era implants—tactile feedback from a military past—glitch him. Technology that was designed for war doesn't cleanly switch off afterward.
- **Economic manipulation as time travel**: The future uses its knowledge of past financial markets to extract massive wealth from stub timelines. Information asymmetry = economic predation.
- **Ainsley Lowbeer**: A London detective (klept) with vastly more power than she lets on. Represents the surviving institutions of the post-Jackpot order—hollowed out but still functional.

## Quotes

> (From H.G. Wells as epigraph): "I have already told you of the sickness and confusion that comes with time travelling."

## My Take

Gibson's most structurally innovative novel. The Jackpot is the most realistic slow-collapse scenario I've read—it's not one thing, it's everything at once, and the wealthy simply buy their way through it while the rest don't. Stub timelines are a cleaner multiverse model than most SF uses. For AI and product thinking: the haptics/PTSD angle is interesting—technology built for one context that persists in another context, glitching. Every "legacy system" is a haptics problem. The peripheral concept (remotely inhabiting a body) anticipates questions about AI embodiment and telerobotics in interesting ways.
