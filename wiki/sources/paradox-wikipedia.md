## Summary

A paradox is a logically self-contradictory statement, or one that leads from apparently valid reasoning and true premises to a seemingly unacceptable or absurd conclusion. Core structural elements include self-reference, contradiction, and infinite regress. W. V. O. Quine classified paradoxes into three types: veridical (surprising but true), falsidical (false due to a hidden fallacy), and antinomies (genuine contradictions arising from accepted reasoning). A fourth type, the dialetheia, is both true and false simultaneously — allowed in paraconsistent logics but denied by classical Aristotelian logic.

## Key Points

- **Self-reference** is a common engine of paradox — "This statement is false" (Liar paradox) recurses infinitely between truth and falsity.
- **Russell's paradox** (the set of all sets that don't contain themselves) showed that naive set theory was flawed, forcing a reexamination of mathematical foundations.
- **Quine's three types**: veridical (Monty Hall, birthday paradox), falsidical (hidden errors), antinomy (Grelling–Nelson paradox about truth and description).
- **Ship of Theseus**: philosophical paradox about identity over time through gradual replacement.
- **Grandfather paradox**: time travel paradox where killing one's own grandfather prevents one's own birth.
- **Paradoxes in medicine**: paradoxical drug reactions (stimulants sedating, sedatives agitating); the smoker's paradox (inverse correlation with certain diseases).
- Carl Jung argued paradox reflects a higher intellect that resists forcing the unknowable into neat categories.
- Kierkegaard held that understanding reality requires embracing paradox rather than rationalism.

## Quotes

> "The paradox reflects a higher level of intellect and, by not forcibly representing the unknowable as known, gives a more faithful picture of the real state of affairs." — Carl Jung

## My Take

Paradoxes are productive. They don't just expose limits — they force expansion. Russell's paradox didn't destroy math; it created type theory and modern logic. The Liar paradox led to formal semantics. I'm most interested in paradoxes as epistemological edge detectors: wherever language or logic breaks, there's a seam in how we've carved up the world.
