## Summary

Scott Pelley interviews Demis Hassabis — co-founder and CEO of Google DeepMind and 2024 Nobel Prize winner in Chemistry — about the state and trajectory of artificial intelligence. Hassabis argues that current definitions of intelligence are incomplete, that AGI remains years away but is the most transformative technology humanity has ever built, and that the best near-term use case for AI is accelerating medical science and human health. He also discusses the philosophical and existential dimensions of AI: what it means for governments, for our understanding of reality, and for safety.

## Key Ideas

- The standard definition of intelligence is not quite right; current AI systems are impressive but still missing key attributes like hierarchical planning, long-term memory, and robust reasoning.
- AGI is the most important and potentially most dangerous technology humans have ever developed — Hassabis treats it with corresponding seriousness.
- AlphaFold (predicting protein structures) was the proof-of-concept moment that AI could achieve something no prior system could: a direct path to curing diseases.
- Hassabis wants to use AI as a scientific instrument to help humanity understand the fundamental nature of reality — physics, consciousness, the universe.
- Governments will inevitably use AI; Hassabis identifies two core concerns: misuse by authoritarian actors and loss of human oversight over increasingly autonomous systems.
- AI safety is not at odds with capability progress — building safe, beneficial AI is the mission, not a constraint on it.
- Hassabis is measured on AGI timelines: probably a handful of years away, not imminent, and current LLMs are not AGI.

## My Take

Hassabis is unusually thoughtful for a sitting CEO at the frontier — he holds the tension between excitement and caution better than most. The framing of AI as a scientific tool (not just a product) is the most interesting thread: using AI to understand reality rather than just to automate tasks is a genuinely different intellectual project. The health/disease framing is compelling but also the safest thing he could say publicly. Would be interesting to hear him pushed harder on the governance and geopolitics angle.
