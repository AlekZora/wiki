## Summary

Isaacson's biography of Leonardo da Vinci draws extensively from Leonardo's 7,200+ pages of surviving notebooks to reconstruct how he thought, worked, and created. Leonardo was illegitimate, self-taught, never finished most projects, and was perpetually curious about things that wouldn't pay—water flow, the way light hits a face, how a woodpecker's tongue works. Yet he produced the Mona Lisa, The Last Supper, and engineering designs centuries ahead of their time. Isaacson argues Leonardo's genius wasn't divine gift but cultivated habit: relentless curiosity, careful observation, and the refusal to separate art from science.

## Key Ideas

- **Curiosity as practice**: Leonardo wrote "tell me if anything was ever done" as a note to himself. He was curious about everything—not strategically, not because it would be useful, but because curiosity was his fundamental mode. His notebooks are full of questions he asked and then investigated.
- **The art-science synthesis**: Leonardo's anatomical studies made him a better painter; his painting made him a better anatomist. He didn't separate these domains because he didn't believe they were separate. The study of light, shadow, proportion, and motion was the same study whether expressed in paint or geometry.
- **Notebooks as thinking tool**: Leonardo's notebooks weren't records of completed thoughts—they were the medium in which he thought. Sketches next to equations next to shopping lists next to engineering diagrams. Thinking by drawing, by annotating, by making.
- **Unfinished as feature**: Leonardo left many works unfinished, not from laziness but because completion interested him less than discovery. The process of finding out was the point; the finished object was incidental.
- **Polymathy as method**: Because Leonardo moved between domains—anatomy, engineering, botany, optics, geology, music—he brought cross-domain analogies to each. His understanding of how water flows in rivers shaped how he painted hair.
- **Sfumato**: Leonardo's technique of painting without hard edges—letting forms blur into each other. A visual philosophy: the world doesn't have hard boundaries; everything transitions into everything else.
- **The Vitruvian Man**: The most famous product of his art-science synthesis—an attempt to reconcile the ideal human proportions of Vitruvius with observed reality by drawing both possibilities simultaneously.

## My Take

The key takeaway is that curiosity is a practice, not a trait. Leonardo wasn't born curious—he cultivated it relentlessly and applied it systematically. For AI product building: the notebook-as-thinking-medium has direct relevance to how AI systems might best assist knowledge workers—not by answering questions but by helping people think *into* problems. The polymathy argument connects to Range (Epstein): breadth enables depth by providing analogical material. For game design: Leonardo's visual observation approach—what does this really look like, how does it actually move—is a model for worldbuilding grounded in physical reality rather than genre convention.
