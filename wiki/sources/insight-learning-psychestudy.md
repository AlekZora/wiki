## Summary

An overview of insight learning as theorized by Wolfgang Köhler, a founder of Gestalt psychology. Insight learning is the sudden realization of a solution to a problem without step-by-step trial and error—exemplified by Sultan the chimpanzee joining two bamboo sticks to reach a banana after a period of apparent inactivity. It involves a pre-solution period of intense engagement, followed by incubation, followed by sudden understanding. Importantly, insight is not random: it depends on prior experience, perceptual reorganization, and understanding causal relationships.

## Key Points

- Coined by Wolfgang Köhler (1887–1967) based on experiments with chimpanzees in Tenerife in the 1910s.
- Sultan experiment: chimp placed in cage with a banana out of reach and two sticks, neither long enough alone. After failed attempts, Sultan joins the sticks during apparent play and suddenly retrieves the banana. On subsequent trials, he solves it immediately.
- Insight learning is distinct from trial-and-error (Thorndike) and stimulus-response conditioning (Pavlov/Watson).
- Characteristics: sudden ("aha" moment), involves perceptual reorganization, not step-by-step, relies on prior experience, more common in higher animals and adults than children.
- Two-phase structure: (1) pre-solution period of intense focused engagement, (2) incubation/idleness during which the solution "pops up."
- Once insight occurs, the solution is retained and applied immediately on re-encounter.
- Some researchers connect insight to associative learning—it may be that unconscious recombination of prior experiences produces the sudden realization.

## Quotes

> "Insight is an awareness of key relationships between cause and effect, which comes after assembling the relevant information and either overt or covert testing of possibilities."

## My Take

Insight learning is the cognitive mechanism behind what people popularly call "aha moments." The two-phase structure (intense work → incubation → sudden realization) matches accounts of creative breakthroughs in mathematics, science, and art—and aligns with what Hofstadter explores in Metamagical Themas about pattern recognition and analogy. The fact that Sultan retained the insight immediately and applied it on the next trial suggests insight creates a qualitatively different kind of memory than rote learning. The key open question: can insight be trained or optimized, or does it emerge as an emergent property of having the right substrate of prior knowledge?
