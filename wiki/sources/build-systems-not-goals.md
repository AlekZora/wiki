## Summary

A coach (a former doctor who says he coaches professionals on learning and careers) argues that people fail at goals because they operate on intentions and plans that quietly depend on willpower and motivation. His alternative is "thinking in systems": build processes that produce results without needing either, then chain working processes together. He gives three principles: think holistically (list every reason the plan will fail, starting from what the person has already tried), build for repeatability (the plan must work on the worst day), and peel the band-aids (temporary patches must eventually be replaced by fixing the underlying cause, and that fix becomes a new goal inside the system). The first two are applied in a loop: find a low-effort option, then ask why it might fail, then revise, until a combination holds.

## Key Ideas

- **Systems reduce dependence on willpower and motivation** rather than trying to supply more of them.
- **Intentions → plans → systems.** A plan ("study after work") is still fragile if it fails on tiredness, traffic or family demands. A system anticipates those.
- **Assume the plan will fail** and enumerate the barriers first; past failed attempts and how the person responded to them are the best data.
- **Repeatability test:** does this plan need willpower on a bad day? If yes, redesign to lower friction.
- **Iterate between "reduce effort" and "what could still break this".** His accountant example: study after work failed, then staying late at the office (beats traffic, but delays family dinner), then morning study (costs sleep, so change the evening routine). The point is that failure of one option doesn't mean no solution exists; a combination does.
- **Discomfort comparison:** a changed routine is uncomfortable, but it is uncomfortable versus the ongoing stress of never progressing, not versus comfort.
- **Good plans contain contingencies** ("if tired, do X; if not, do Y").
- **Peel the band-aids:** naps and timers help now but bloat the system and add conditions for success; treat fixing sleep and attention span as a goal in its own right.

## My Take

**Anecdotal, coaching-industry source.** The evidence is the speaker's own client stories, so treat the principles as a useful engineering heuristic rather than tested findings. It also ends with a newsletter pitch.

**AI lens:**

1. *Willpower is the human analogue of "the model will try harder."* A plan that only works if the operator (human or LLM) is at their best is the same failure as an agent that only works when the prompt is perfect and the context clean. The fix in both cases is structural: lower friction, constrain the path, make the default action the correct one. This connects to [harness-engineering](../concepts/harness-engineering.md).
2. *Enumerate failure modes first, then redesign.* It is red-teaming a plan before running it; the back-and-forth between "reduce effort" and "what breaks this" resembles a critic loop, and the whole procedure is a [closed-loop system](../concepts/closed-loop-systems.md) applied to one's own behavior.
3. *Band-aids vs. root cause* maps directly onto prompt patches. Each special-case instruction added to a prompt to cover a failure is a band-aid that adds conditions for success; the durable fix changes the underlying capability or environment. His advice to make removal of the band-aid an explicit tracked goal is one I haven't seen stated for agent systems.
4. *AI as a system-design partner for people:* the iterate-over-barriers process is exactly what an LLM could run with someone interactively (ask what they've tried, propose low-effort combinations, probe failure). Whether it would push through discomfort as the coach describes, rather than accept the first plausible plan, is the open question.
