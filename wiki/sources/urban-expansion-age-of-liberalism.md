## Summary

Between 1800 and 1914 Western cities expanded at rates that now look impossible: Berlin's
metropolitan population grew twentyfold, Manchester's twenty-five, New York's a hundred,
Sydney's around 240 and Toronto's perhaps 1,700. Chicago grew roughly five thousand times
between 1833 and 1900 — doubling every five years on average. Raw population understates
it, because homes simultaneously got larger, held fewer people, and sat on a smaller share
of land as boulevards, parks and railways took up the rest. Through all of this real house
prices stayed roughly flat while incomes doubled or tripled, so British house prices fell
from about twelve times average earnings in 1850 to about four times in 1914. The cities
also got *better*: wider and better-connected streets, vast transit networks, and the
mixed use and gentle density that urban designers now fight for. The article's central
claim is that this was not laissez-faire, contrary to how the period is described by both
its admirers and its detractors. Streets and drains were planned and funded publicly, at
least as activistically as today. Transport and utilities were deliberately organised as
regulated monopolies, in three variants — franchise, concession, and municipal ownership —
all of which excluded competitors, regulated prices, and funded themselves through user
fees rather than taxes while aiming to turn a profit. What *was* liberal was the treatment
of individual buildings: the right to build was close to axiomatic, regulation covered
structure and fire rather than aesthetics or use, and height limits applied uniformly
across a whole jurisdiction rather than varying by neighborhood. The unifying principle,
the author concludes, was not minimal government but the alignment of private interest with
public good — "users pay", "suppliers profit", and "spendthrift public bodies go bankrupt."

## Key Points

- **Extension plans made landowners build the city's street network for it.** Authorities
  drew maps of future streets before development arrived — the Commissioners' Plan for
  Manhattan in 1811, the Plan Cerdà for Barcelona's Eixample, and roughly 100 cities
  planned by Josef Stübben alone. The dominant enforcement model, used in Germany, Italy
  and Spain, banned development on the designated alignments and conditioned development
  of the remaining land on landowners grading the alignments, part-funding their surfacing,
  and ceding them to the authorities. Because development was highly profitable, this made
  implementing the city's plan the landowners' own best move. Compulsory purchase was
  reserved for arterials and holdouts.
- **Street standards were extravagant by the standards of actual demand.** Minimum widths:
  12 metres in London, 22 in Berlin, 18 for New York's cross streets and 30 for its
  avenues — set when very few people owned carriages and those who did stored them
  off-street. London banned cul-de-sacs longer than 60 feet outright, because the
  value-maximising street type for a single developer (excluding through traffic) is
  ruinous if everyone chooses it.
- **Drains were the one pure public good and were always publicly funded.** The benefits —
  clean groundwater, less flooding, fewer epidemics — were unsellable to individuals, so
  private enterprise never played more than a marginal role. The gong farmers who profited
  from the cesspit system lobbied against sewerage; in Düsseldorf they succeeded in barring
  the expensive new system from carrying human waste for about twenty years.
- **Two market failures drove the monopoly system.** Transport generates positive
  spillovers the operator cannot capture — a railway can charge you for visiting your
  grandmother but not her for receiving you — so the market undersupplies it. And
  **Hotelling's Law** pushes competitors to converge rather than differentiate: rival
  operators duplicate each other's coverage instead of spacing out. Kent's two railway
  companies built largely duplicative networks, merged in 1899, and left southeast London
  with an inefficient layout that persists today. Competing bus companies "raced" each
  other to reach waiting passengers first.
- **Three monopoly variants, one shared logic.** *Franchise* (dominant in the US): private
  ownership, municipally regulated prices and routes, time-limited. *Concession* (France,
  emulated in Spain and Italy): municipality owns the physical infrastructure, private
  operator runs it for a term — the two dominant French firms are now Veolia and Suez.
  *Municipalization* (pioneered in the German Empire): the city owns and runs everything,
  unified under *Stadtwerke*. In all three, competitors were excluded, prices regulated,
  and services funded by user fees, with profit expected — municipal works were often
  established *because* they were lucrative, funding other services or tax cuts.
  Municipalities were not bailed out by national governments, so bankruptcy discipline was
  real.
- **The undertaking-debt pattern.** Fares ran high for ten or twenty years while the
  operator repaid installation debt, after which caps were lowered towards operating cost.
  Boston's gas prices were cut in 1899 on this basis; Cleveland's tram franchise had the
  mechanism written in automatically; Glasgow cut tram fares steadily after paying off its
  debt in the 1910s.
- **Inflation destroyed the system, not any flaw in its design.** There was essentially no
  inflation before 1914, so a price cap set once at a reasonable level stayed sustainable
  indefinitely and could even improve with productivity. When inflation resumed in 1914 the
  real value of user fees collapsed; by the 1920s most public transport companies were near
  ruin, entering the era of automobile competition financially crippled. Utilities were
  obviously essential enough that governments eventually raised their prices; transport
  mostly did not recover. German trams survived where British, French and American ones
  died because *Stadtwerke* could cross-subsidize them from still-profitable utilities.
- **Buildings were the genuinely liberal part.** The right to build was axiomatic —
  *Baufreiheit* was treated as a principle of political liberalism in German-speaking
  Europe and simply assumed in the Anglophone world. Codes governed structural soundness
  and fire safety; European rules banning timber facades are why much German urban fabric
  survived the war while Japan's timber cities burned.
- **Height limits were uniform, and that mattered enormously.** Paris capped at about eight
  storeys, Berlin five, Vienna and Rome six — but the same limit applied across an entire
  jurisdiction rather than varying by neighborhood. Since no municipality was willing to ban
  mid-rise centrally, mid-rise was permitted everywhere, which kept suburban densification
  and dense greenfield development permanently available. Differential area zoning only
  emerged in Germany in the 1890s.
- **Beauty was never regulated.** Codes almost never required ornament, good materials, or
  proportion; a compliant ugly building was entirely possible. The belief that Haussmann
  controlled Parisian architectural style is basically untrue — the authorities chose a
  style only for the minority of streets they had compulsorily purchased, acting as
  landowner rather than regulator. London's four "rates" of house governed construction
  standards and said nothing about facades. The conclusion is that developers invested in
  beauty under market pressure, because people wanted it enough to pay for it despite being
  poor.
- **Unrestricted supply kept prices at land-plus-build-cost.** Real house prices stayed flat
  across the century despite enormous demand growth, and price per square metre actually
  fell as agricultural land values dropped under New World and Ukrainian grain competition
  and build costs fell with factory-produced brick, joinery and ornament. Postwar, building
  restrictions explain roughly 80 percent of price increases in Europe, Canada and
  Australia; Britain reached nine times earnings in 2020, the same ratio as 1876.
- **The system undersupplied one thing: stability.** Permissiveness made neighborhood
  character constantly vulnerable, and middle-class buyers paid premiums for privately
  covenanted areas that promised to freeze it. The covenants were largely ineffective, but
  the willingness to pay suggests nineteenth-century governments were offering *less*
  development management than would have maximised value.

> would not be laissez-faire, but rather the alignment of private interests and public good."

> 'suppliers profit', and 'spendthrift public bodies go bankrupt'."

> "The conclusion is unavoidable that developers invested in beauty not because of
> regulatory pressures but because of market ones. To put that differently,
> nineteenth-century people got beautiful buildings because they wanted them – enough that
> they were prepared to pay for them, despite their relative poverty."

> "Nineteenth-century cities were materially poor and technologically primitive, but their
> institutions were often flexible, vigorous, and creative. We still have something to learn
> from them."

## My Take

**The extension-plan mechanism is the cleanest piece of mechanism design in the article and
the most transferable.** The city neither built the streets nor compelled anyone to build
them. It published a map, forbade building on the lines, and made everything else
conditional on grading and ceding them — after which constructing the public network became
the profit-maximising move for every individual landowner. That is a constraint structure,
not an instruction set, and it is the shape you want for agent systems: you do not get good
global behavior by removing all limits, nor by specifying every action centrally, but by
arranging the environment so the locally-optimising agent produces the globally good outcome
as a side effect. Most agent scaffolds I have seen reach for the instruction set first.

**Hotelling's Law names a multi-agent failure mode this wiki did not have a word for.**
Independent competitors converge on the middle rather than differentiating, duplicating
coverage and leaving the space badly served — two railways down the same Kentish corridor,
buses racing each other to the same stop. That is precisely what happens when you run
several LLM agents on the same objective over the same input and expect diversity: they
converge, and the parallelism buys almost nothing. The nineteenth-century answer was to
grant one operator the whole network so that adding a service meant *complementing* its
existing services rather than cannibalising them. The agent analogue is that explicit
territory or role assignment beats independent competition whenever the goal is coverage —
worth adding to the vocabulary alongside `multi-agent-orchestration` and
`swarm-intelligence`.

**The inflation collapse is the sharpest thing here.** The system did not fail because it
was badly designed. It failed because it had silently encoded an assumption about the world
— that prices do not move — into a constant, and then the world moved. A fare cap set once
was permanently sustainable in a zero-inflation economy and became lethal within a decade in
an inflationary one, and no amount of testing the system against itself would have surfaced
that, because the flaw was not in the system. This is a genuinely useful lens for AI
engineering, where hardcoded constants are everywhere: eval thresholds, token budgets,
timeout values, "good enough" latency targets, context-window assumptions, cost-per-call
arithmetic. Each one embeds a snapshot of a fast-moving environment. The failure mode to
watch for is not the parameter being wrong when set, but the parameter being right when set
and never being re-derived. Something like an explicit inventory of "constants whose
correctness depends on facts about the world in 2026" would be cheap and probably valuable.

**And the beauty finding complements the concept extracted from this article's companion
piece today.** Regulation reliably produced structural soundness and fire safety; it never
produced beauty, and was never even attempted at it. Beauty came from demand — people
wanting it enough to pay while poor. Read against `diffuse-preference-aggregation`, which
covers how distant low-stakes preferences acquire veto power, the pair suggests a real
asymmetry: aggregated cursory preference is quite good at *blocking* things and quite bad at
*causing* good ones. Safety and correctness are producible by constraint; quality is not. That
maps uncomfortably well onto what RLHF does and does not deliver.
