## Summary

Rachel Thomas pushes back against the growing narrative that screen time is inherently harmful for children. Drawing on her family's homeschooling experience, she argues that "screen time" is a meaninglessly broad category that lumps passive TV watching together with composing music, writing code, and connecting with friends. She calls for nuance, challenges false binaries, and highlights how online options serve children whose needs aren't met by traditional schools.

## Key Points

- "Screen time" conflates wildly different activities — the relevant dimensions are: social vs. solitary, creating vs. consuming, educational vs. not.
- Online learning has enabled her daughter to have tutors in 5 countries, take advanced science classes, and compose music collaboratively with long-distance friends.
- Many children (neurodivergent, gifted, medically complex, bullied, or from marginalized cultures) are poorly served by in-person school — online options can be essential.
- Punitive attendance policies that require sick children in school are counterproductive and harmful.
- The push for office return and in-person school attendance are politically and economically linked — both require working parents to commute.
- Screens are a crucial tool for accessibility and disability rights; options that improved in 2020 are now being rolled back.

## Quotes

> "The term 'screen time' is so broad as to be meaningless, clumping together many disparate activities."

> "Our friendships aren't any less real because they are over screens."

## My Take

The Venn diagram framework (social/solitary × creating/consuming × educational/not) is a genuinely useful heuristic. The argument is strongest when Rachel connects it to disability and accessibility — framing remote options as a rights issue, not a convenience one. The political dimension (office return ↔ school attendance ↔ worker mobility) is pointed and probably underdiscussed.
