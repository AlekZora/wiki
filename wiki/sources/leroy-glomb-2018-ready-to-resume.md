This page is built from the university's press release, which quotes the paper and the lead
author. Effect sizes are not available here.

## Summary

When people are interrupted under time pressure, their mind stays partly on the unfinished task.
Leroy calls this **attention residue**, and it hurts performance on the interrupting task. A
"ready-to-resume plan" fixes much of it: about a minute spent noting where you left off and how
you'll pick back up. Across four studies (a survey of 202 working professionals, then three lab
studies, two of them with 66 and 44 students), writing the plan reduced attention residue. It
also improved decisions and recall on the *new* task. **The studies did not test whether the plan
helps when people return to the original task.** Leroy says she sees no reason it wouldn't.

## Key Points

- **Attention residue:** an unfinished, time-pressured task keeps "windows open" in the mind and degrades focus on the next task.
- **The plan's contents** (quoting the paper): "where to resume, what challenges are left, and/or what actions (you) must postpone but resume later." Short. "Even a minute's work will do."
- **What was measured:** residue during, and performance on, the *interrupting* task (Task B): better decisions and better recall of résumé details.
- **What was not measured:** performance on the interrupted task (Task A) after returning.
- **Samples:** one field survey (n = 202) and lab studies with students (the two plan-testing studies had n = 66 and n = 44).

## Quotes

> "The studies did not explore whether the plan improved performance on the interrupted Task A when participants returned to it."

> "It's like Windows staying open in our brains, and it makes it hard to focus on the intervening work." (Sophie Leroy)

## My Take

This is the most important correction from today's checks. The [research report](evidence-based-goal-achievement-system.md) calls the ready-to-resume plan its "most defensible distinctive micro-intervention". It builds a resume screen and a "resume latency" metric on top of it. But the evidence is about **letting go of a task cleanly**, not **getting back into it faster**. Both may well be true. Only the first has been tested, in small lab samples.

For a goal app, the safe claim is "write the next step so you can stop thinking about it." The unproven claim is "write the next step and you'll restart faster."

**AI lens:** this is [cognitive externalization](../concepts/cognitive-externalization.md) for a person. Moving task state out of working memory into a note frees capacity for the next thing, the same reason agents write state to external memory before switching context. The agent analogy also shows the untested half. For an agent, a good handoff note clearly speeds resumption, because the state is gone otherwise. For a human, residue is the stronger effect, and resumption speed is still an open question. A product could test it directly by measuring time from reopening to first meaningful action, with and without the note.
