## Summary

A philosophy academic explains why astrology has surged in popularity among Gen Z. As
religious affiliation declines, astrology offers a "post-traditional, post-religious"
sense of meaning: connection to something beyond the material world, without doctrine
or institutional membership. Social media (TikTok, Instagram) made astrology
ambient and interactive rather than something actively sought out, turning it into a
digital spiritual community that functions like the belonging traditional religious
spaces used to provide. Astrology varies widely across cultures (Western, Chinese,
Hindu/Jyotisha, Islamic, Tibetan) and Gen Z's cultural openness may broaden its appeal
beyond the Western zodiac. Key stats: TikTok has 4.5M+ videos tagged 'astrology'
(Jan 2025); Co-Star grew from 7.5M to 30M users (2020–2023); the mystical-services
spending market is projected to grow from $12.8B to $22.8B (2021–2031); UK theistic
identification fell from 75% (1981) to 49% (2023). The piece closes by asking what
organized religion could learn from astrology's accessibility, personalization, and
flexibility.

## Key Points

- Astrology fills a meaning-and-belonging gap left by declining religious affiliation,
  while requiring no doctrinal commitment.
- Social media transformed astrology from something sought out to something ambient —
  it appears in the feed unprompted and invites engagement (horoscopes, compatibility
  posts, memes).
- Astrology is not one system: Western, Chinese, Hindu, Islamic, and Tibetan astrology
  each encode different cosmologies and serve different cultural functions.
- Engagement is mostly playful/self-reflective rather than doctrinal belief — the
  author argues astrology can coexist with critical thinking as an introspection tool.
- The author's ask of religion: match astrology's digital accessibility, personalization,
  and low-commitment openness without abandoning core doctrine.

## Quotes

> "Astrology offers an alternative, post-traditional, and post-religious sense of
> meaning. It provides a way to engage with something beyond the material world while
> remaining flexible and personal."

> "In a way, astrology has taken on the role of a digital spiritual community, providing
> a sense of connection that previous generations might have found in traditional
> religious spaces."

## My Take

The AI intersection here is direct: what astrology is doing structurally — ambient,
personalized, algorithmically-delivered meaning-content with zero institutional
membership cost — is exactly the shape of product an LLM-based companion or "digital
oracle" is positioned to occupy, and arguably already does for some users. The appeal
isn't the predictive content (few users report literal belief) but the interaction
structure: a system that appears to know something about *you specifically* and
returns individualized guidance on demand. A chatbot that reflects a user's stated
values back to them in personalized language is functionally adjacent to a horoscope —
both provide a low-commitment interpretive frame a user can apply to their own life,
without requiring the user to submit to an external authority. This raises the same
question the article poses for religion, aimed at AI instead: as LLM companions absorb
more of this meaning-making function, do they inherit its risks (the "woo-woo washing"
critique — validating anything the user wants to hear) along with its appeal?
