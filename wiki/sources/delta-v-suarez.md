## Summary

James Tighe, an extreme cave diver, is recruited by a secretive billionaire named Nathan Joyce for a private asteroid mining mission to Ryugu. The novel covers the selection and training of the crew, the voyage, and the engineering and political challenges of the first attempt to extract material from a near-Earth asteroid. Suarez is known for technical rigor—the physics, economics, and engineering of asteroid mining are portrayed with unusual accuracy.

## Key Ideas

- **Delta-v economics**: Delta-v (change in velocity) is the real currency of space—more important than time or money. Every maneuver costs delta-v; the entire design of the mission is constrained by it. The title refers to the fundamental physical budget of space travel.
- **Private space as moral argument**: Joyce frames the mission as the only path to human survival—governments are too slow, too politically constrained, too short-horizon. A private actor with long-term thinking and willingness to take asymmetric risk is the only way to bootstrap a space economy.
- **Crew selection as design**: The novel is detailed about how you select people for a multi-year mission with no resupply and minimal communication. Skills, psychological stability, ability to resolve conflict, tolerance for isolation. A worked example of team composition for extreme circumstances.
- **You can't cross a chasm in two small jumps**: The epigraph sets the book's theme—incremental approaches to existential challenges don't work. Asteroid mining requires a commitment to a genuinely hard problem, not an easier version of it.
- **Resource extraction as civilization foundation**: The argument Suarez is making is that material abundance (starting with metals from asteroids) unlocks everything else. Energy, climate, conflict—all downstream of resource scarcity.
- **Self-selection of the committed**: Joyce uses extreme physical and psychological challenges (cave diving in collapsing systems) to pre-filter candidates—only people who understand genuine risk and still choose to proceed are worth bringing.

## Quotes

> "You can't cross a chasm in two small jumps." (epigraph, David Lloyd George)

## My Take

Delta-v is technically the most accurate near-future space novel I've read. The "delta-v as currency" framing is a useful cognitive tool—every system has a finite budget of something (attention, computation, capital) and design is fundamentally about allocation. The crew selection problem is interesting for AI teams: what's the delta-v equivalent in a high-stakes AI development context? For product building: the "you can't cross in two small jumps" principle argues against minimum-viable versions of problems that require full commitment. Connect to Rework's "launch now" principle—they're in tension, usefully.
