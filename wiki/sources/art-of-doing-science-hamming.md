## Summary

Hamming, the inventor of error-correcting codes and a legend at Bell Labs, spent decades asking why some scientists and engineers do great work and most don't. This book is the transcription of the course he taught at the Naval Postgraduate School—ostensibly about digital filters, coding theory, and simulation, but actually about how to think. His thesis: greatness in science and engineering is not genius, it's a *style* of thinking that can be learned. The technical content is just the vehicle; the real subject is how to approach problems, careers, and knowledge in a way that lets you transcend your times.

## Key Ideas

- **Style of thinking over technical content**: The technical examples (digital filters, Fourier transforms, coding theory) are just illustrations. The real lesson is the *style*—how great scientists approach problems, question assumptions, and build mental models.
- **"You and Your Research"**: His most famous lecture. Great scientists work on important problems. They know what the important problems are in their field. They have courage to pursue them and the drive to remove the obstacles between them and the solution.
- **Important problems**: Ask yourself weekly: what are the most important problems in my field? If what you're working on isn't one of them, why not? If you can't answer why an important problem can't be worked on, you're not paying attention.
- **Luck favors the prepared mind**: Great results cluster in the same people not by chance but because preparation determines who can recognize and act on opportunities.
- **Open doors**: Hamming deliberately kept his office door open. Closed-door workers did more work but less great work—they optimized locally. Open-door workers learned from the environment.
- **The emotional investment**: You must truly care about the problem. Emotional commitment to a problem is what sustains the effort required for a breakthrough.
- **Learning to learn**: The half-life of engineering knowledge is getting shorter. The ability to learn new things is more valuable than any specific thing you currently know.
- **Great work as public gift**: Hamming's conception of great work is work that becomes teachable—information theory, error-correcting codes, the FFT. Given freely to humanity to be learned and built upon, not hidden behind an API.

## My Take

Hamming's framework for identifying and working on important problems is directly applicable to AI research today. The question "what are the important problems in my field, and why am I not working on them?" is a useful gut-check for anyone building AI products or doing research. His concept of greatness-as-practice (not genius) connects to Range (Epstein) and Mindstorms (Papert)—the idea that how you approach things matters more than what you start with. For product building: his "open door" philosophy is an argument for working in public, building in public, and staying exposed to real problems.
