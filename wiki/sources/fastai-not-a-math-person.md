## Summary

Rachel Thomas (math PhD, fast.ai co-founder) makes the case that "not being a math person" is a cultural construct, not a biological fact. She traces the myths of innate ability and the lone genius, the structural racism and sexism baked into math education, the toxic culture of math academia, and the curricular failures that teach math as an accumulation of disconnected techniques rather than a coherent "whole game." The piece is an appeal to reform not just who gets access to math, but how math is taught and experienced.

## Key Points

- The myth of innate ability ("wired for math or not") has no research support, but when people believe it, it becomes self-fulfilling. Dr. Omoju Miller's research debunks this directly.
- The "lone genius" myth: math actually depends on collaboration and incremental community work, not solitary brilliance (Cathy O'Neil, Harvard Math PhD).
- Paul Lockhart's analogy: imagine if children couldn't sing or play instruments until adulthood, after years of transcribing sheet music. That's how math is taught — the creative and interesting parts are withheld until nearly everyone has dropped out.
- David Perkins: math teaches the "whole game" too late. Kids can play a junior version of baseball; math should offer a junior version of actual mathematical thinking.
- Math curricula are arranged vertically — one bad year (bad teacher, illness, ADHD) poisons everything that follows. Other subjects are more horizontally forgiving.
- Female teachers' math anxiety measurably reduces girls' math achievement. Structural racism shapes which students are expected to succeed.
- Academic math culture is "an absolute mess which actively pushes out the sort of people who might make it better" — Dr. Piper Harron, whose Princeton PhD thesis made waves for being deliberately accessible.

## Quotes

> "My view of mathematics is that it is an absolute mess which actively pushes out the sort of people who might make it better." — Dr. Piper Harron

> "You don't have to be a genius to become a mathematician. If you find this statement at all surprising, you're an example of what's wrong with the way our society identifies, encourages and rewards talent." — Dr. Cathy O'Neil

## My Take

This article reads like a companion to Lockhart's Lament — both argue that math education is pedagogically inverted. Thomas adds the gender/race dimension and the personal testimony angle. The overlap with whole-game learning is direct: the fix isn't just about access or representation, it's about changing what counts as math in a classroom.
