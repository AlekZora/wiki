## Summary

A response essay in a *Cato Unbound* issue whose lead essay, by Stephen Davies, asked how
the modern world diverged so rapidly from the pre-modern one. Goldstone argues that the
"great divergence" was not the flowering of something latent in Western culture but a
repudiation of most of it, and that its crux was what he calls a **marriage of engineering
culture and entrepreneurship**. Engineering culture is a specific cluster of new beliefs
about knowledge: that reliable knowledge of the material world comes from empirical
research programs using ever more precise instruments, rather than from pure reason,
mathematics, unaided observation of nature, or authoritative ancient texts; that such
instruments make previously abstract quantities — energy, work, power, heat, motion —
measurable; that with those measurements one can design steadily more powerful machines
and discover new materials and processes; and that this would produce a future age of
well-being greater than any known. That last belief inverted the near-universal
assumption, including in the classical and medieval West, that humanity's golden age lay
in the past. Knowledge alone was insufficient: it had to be married to an elite belief
that applying engineering skill to production, transport and communication was a superior
way to make profits in competitive markets — the world of Watt, Boulton and the Lunar
Society. That marriage in turn required social supports: rulers had to stop enforcing the
knowledge claims of religious and traditional authorities, and had to give up controlling
private firms' access to knowledge and markets, which meant weakening guilds and
overturning absolute royal authority. Goldstone's conclusion is a policy one. "Western
society" and "liberal society" are different things, and the second was a radical
departure from the first; countries seeking Western growth need not adopt Greco-Roman,
Christian or Renaissance inheritance, but do need both halves — liberal social order *and*
science-driven private entrepreneurship. Supporting engineering while resisting liberalism
as a foreign intrusion is not a formula for success.

## Key Points

- **Engineering culture is four beliefs, not a technology.** Empirical instrument-driven
  research as the source of reliable material knowledge; measurement of previously
  abstract quantities; design of ever-better machines from those measurements; and a
  future age of abundance resulting from it.
- **The temporal inversion is the load-bearing belief.** Almost all civilizations,
  including the classical and medieval West, located the golden age in the past.
  Engineering culture placed an earthly paradise in the future, reached by human progress
  rather than divine redemption. Goldstone quotes Joseph Priestley to this effect.
- **Knowledge without commercial application does nothing.** It "would do little if it
  remained confined to laboratories and academic societies." The second half of the
  marriage is an elite belief in technological entrepreneurship.
- **State-led technical achievement is real but does not generalize.** Goldstone grants
  the U.S. space program and China's mag-lev trains, but argues such efforts do not
  produce the economy-wide dynamism that comes from empowering entrepreneurs across all
  forms of enterprise.
- **Two political preconditions.** Rulers had to stop enforcing religious and traditional
  knowledge claims, which produced state-approved religious pluralism and open education;
  and they had to stop controlling firms' access to knowledge and markets, which meant
  reducing guild and urban-government authority. Both required a limited state.
- **Small states got there first.** Britain, Holland, Prussia and Denmark found they
  gained more from accommodating varied beliefs than from enforcing conformity. Larger
  states paid a high economic price for stability — France expelling the Huguenots, Spain
  its Jews — and the major Asian empires met disorder by reinforcing orthodoxy.
- **This was a repudiation of the Western past, not its fruit.** Greece and Rome had
  citizenship as a privileged status compatible with slavery and chattel treatment of
  women and children. Frankish "freedom" was the ordinary character of unstratified
  nomadic societies, from the Mongols to Native Americans. Imperial Roman law was the
  antithesis of a limited state. The trading oligarchies of Venice, Genoa and Holland
  recognized no natural rights and protected enterprise through tight political control of
  markets and routes.
- **Pre-modern Europe was not even the innovation leader in products.** Indian producers
  of printed and woven cottons and Chinese ceramic makers dominated international markets
  more effectively than Venetian glass-blowers or Flemish weavers. What none of them had
  was the notion of scientific research programs by free thinkers as the basis of future
  material progress.
- **"Rise of the West" is a misnomer.** Goldstone reframes the great divergence as the
  first phase of the eclipse of societies based on closed authoritarian hierarchies, which
  simply began with Western ones — invoking North, Wallis and Weingast's "open access
  orders." What lies ahead is not a clash of civilizations but a continuing clash between
  liberal social order and closed hierarchies.

## My Take

Goldstone is describing the *first* instance of a belief this wiki has been accumulating
recent instances of without noticing they are the same belief.

**The golden-age inversion is the AGI expectation, verbatim.** Goldstone's fourth
component — that human progress in applying new knowledge will produce a future age of
well-being beyond anything previously known, replacing divine redemption as the mechanism
of paradise — is almost exactly what the Hassabis piece ingested on 2026-07-17
(`frontier-ai-standards-body-hassabis.md`) argues about AGI: effects roughly 10x the
Industrial Revolution at 10x the speed, unlocking a post-scarcity "era of abundance."
Priestley in the eighteenth century and Hassabis in 2026 are making structurally identical
claims, and Goldstone's argument is that this belief is not decoration on top of the
technical work but a precondition for anyone bothering to do it. That is a genuinely
useful frame for reading current AGI discourse: the forecasts function partly as
coordination devices, licensing investment and effort, regardless of their accuracy. It
also sets up a sharper question than "will the forecast come true" — namely, what happened
the last time an elite adopted this belief, and how much of the subsequent growth was
caused by the belief itself.

**The complementarity claim cuts against pure-capability accounts of AI-driven growth.**
Goldstone's central move is that the knowledge revolution was necessary but nowhere near
sufficient — it needed entrepreneurship, which needed a limited state, which needed the
overthrow of guild and religious authority, and the whole chain had to hold at once. Read
against `diminishing-research-returns` (from the Bloom/Jones/Van Reenen/Webb paper), the
two make an interesting pair: that paper says ideas are getting harder to find, while
Goldstone implies the binding constraint was never idea production but the institutional
machinery that converts ideas into deployed capability. If he is right, an AI system that
massively accelerates idea generation is pushing on the input that was not scarce. The
claim that state-led programs achieve real things but not economy-wide dynamism is the
uncomfortable version of this for anyone expecting frontier-lab capability alone to produce
broad productivity growth.

**Measurement as the enabling move is the eval problem.** Belief two — that instruments
made energy, work, power and heat into measurable quantities — is the hinge of the whole
programme, because belief three (design better machines from those measurements) is
impossible without it. That is an unusually clean historical statement of the principle
underneath modern ML practice: capabilities that cannot be measured cannot be
systematically improved, and progress in the field tracks the history of its benchmarks
closely. It is the constructive complement to `metrics-trap`, which covers what goes wrong
when the measure becomes the target. The genuinely open question the pairing raises is what
the current equivalent of "energy" or "work" is for AI — which presently-abstract quality
of a model is awaiting the instrument that would make it measurable, and therefore
engineerable. Reasoning quality, situational awareness and deception are the obvious
candidates, and none of them yet has a thermometer.
