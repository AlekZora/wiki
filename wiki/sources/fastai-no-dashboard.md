## Summary

Rachel Thomas argues that AI edtech often amplifies the failures of traditional schooling rather than fixing them — primarily by doubling down on quantification and skill decomposition. When an edtech entrepreneur pitched a learning dashboard to track her daughter's proficiencies, Thomas and her husband (Jeremy Howard) actively refused it. The article traces how high-stakes standardized testing, reductive ELA curricula, and Goodhart's Law have degraded education for decades, and how AI risks entrenching these same patterns.

## Key Points

- The "tyranny of metrics" in education: when the measure becomes the target, it ceases to be a good measure (Goodhart's Law). AI is especially dangerous here because it optimizes metrics too effectively.
- US ELA curricula now decompose reading into discrete testable skills (decoding, summarizing, inferencing) while 3rd–6th graders often go a whole year without reading a novel. Reading for fun among 9-year-olds dropped from 53% to 39% between 2012–2022.
- Texas under George W. Bush was the birthplace of high-stakes standardized testing; this spread nationwide via No Child Left Behind. The "Texas Miracle" was later debunked by a 60 Minutes investigation.
- Teachers forced into data-collection roles report burnout and loss of autonomy. An Australian teacher described feeling like "an assessor, an examiner, a data collector" rather than a teacher.
- AI edtech currently exacerbates these problems: more granular metrics, less relationship, less whole-game engagement.
- fast.ai's counter-model: keep humans at the center, build community (forums, Discord, in-person meetups), let students share creative projects with no fixed theme.

## Quotes

> "I actively do not want that." — Jeremy Howard, on a learning dashboard for their daughter

> "I'm rarely required to 'teach' anymore. Apparently I'm more valuable as an assessor, an examiner, a data collector." — Gabbie Stroud, Australian teacher

> "Not only do I despise forcing poorly written, artificial texts onto my students, but I really hate feeling like my personal pedagogy is being dampened." — Colorado teacher, 2025

## My Take

The argument is basically Goodhart's Law applied to education, which is well-trodden ground — but Thomas makes it sharp by connecting it to AI specifically. The danger isn't that AI causes these problems; it's that AI makes a pre-existing pathology worse. The most interesting move is the refusal to treat "analog school" as the baseline of health — she's equally critical of both analog and AI approaches that prioritize measurable decomposition over engagement.
