chapter's own.

## Summary

The classic meta-analysis behind "if–then plans". Holding a strong goal intention ("I intend to
reach Z") does not guarantee you act, because goal striving runs into self-regulation problems
along the way. An **implementation intention** is a second act of will: "If situation Y occurs,
then I will initiate behavior X." Across 94 independent tests, forming one had a
medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d = .65). It helped with all four problems the authors
name: getting started, staying on track against distractions and unwanted responses,
disengaging from failing courses of action, and conserving self-regulatory capacity. The
mechanism had strong support. The plan makes the cue more noticeable ("accessibility") and makes
the response to it more automatic.

## Key Points

- **d = .65 across 94 tests** (p. 69). The result held across study designs, outcome measures and goal domains (p. 109).
- **Four problems, all helped** (pp. 69, 109): initiating, shielding ongoing pursuit, **disengaging from failing goals**, and preserving capacity for future striving. The evidence was thinner for disengagement and conservation: 52 and 21 studies covered initiation and shielding, fewer covered the other two (p. 104).
- **Mechanism** (p. 106): the plan raises the accessibility of the critical cue and strengthens the cue → response link. Webb & Sheeran (2005c) found both mediated goal attainment.
- **Rehearsal beats a reminder note** (p. 108): Milne & Sheeran (2002c). People who mentally rehearsed the cue–response link acted on their plan **87%** of the time. People who put the written plan somewhere visible as a reminder: **40%**. No plan: **20%**.
- **From plan to habit** (p. 108): repeated action on an if–then plan produced more self-reported features of habitual control than acting on a goal intention alone (Orbell & Verplanken, 2005).
- **Rigidity is mostly not a problem** (p. 104): if–then planners still notice and use alternative good opportunities. **Open:** what happens when a person realizes their plan was faulty. Do they stick to it, modify it, or stop planning altogether?
- **Who it might backfire for** (p. 107): people poor at reality monitoring may form plans that work against them. **Perfectionists** who "set too much store by adherence to plans" may ruminate. Those who already plan spontaneously gain less.
- **Limits of the evidence** (p. 105): mostly laboratory tasks and undergraduate samples. Health studies were mostly about *starting* protective behaviors, not stopping risky ones.
- **Examples by problem** (Appendix I, p. 110): "If it is Saturday morning at 10 a.m., then I will sit down at my computer and make an outline for my essay!" for overcoming reluctance to start.

## Quotes

> "Holding a strong goal intention ('I intend to reach Z!') does not guarantee goal achievement, because people may fail to deal effectively with self-regulatory problems during goal striving." (p. 69)

> "Medium-to-large effects were obtained in relation to initiating goal striving, shielding goals from unwanted influences, disengaging from failing goals, and preserving self-regulatory capability for future goal striving." (p. 109)

> "Do people stubbornly adhere to the faulty if–then plan, or readily modify the if- and then-components of that plan, or do they even completely refrain from forming if–then plans?" (p. 104)

## My Take

This confirms the headline claim in the [goal-achievement research report](evidence-based-goal-achievement-system.md) (d = .65, 94 tests). It also adds two things the report left out, and both matter more for design than the effect size does. **A reminder is not rehearsal:** a written plan placed as a reminder roughly doubled action, but rehearsing the link roughly quadrupled it. And **if–then plans help people let go of failing goals**, not only persist. That makes "retire this goal" a supported outcome, not a defeat.

**AI lens:** an implementation intention is a trigger–action rule: a condition the system watches for, bound to a response it fires without deliberating again. That is how event-driven agents and harness hooks already work. The finding that the binding has to be *encoded*, not just *stored*, has a human-side lesson for AI coaches. An assistant that writes the user's if–then plan for them and sends it as a reminder is doing the 40% version. One that gets the user to state and rehearse the link is aiming at the 87% version. That claim comes from one study (Milne & Sheeran) in a lab-era evidence base. It is promising, not settled. See [implementation-intentions](../concepts/implementation-intentions.md).
