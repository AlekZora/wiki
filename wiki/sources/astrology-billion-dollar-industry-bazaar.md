## Summary

A culture-desk piece tracking astrology's turn from fringe interest to mainstream
economy. The "mystical services" market (astrology, tarot, mediumship, palm reading)
is estimated at ~$2.2B globally, growing ~0.5%/year since 2017. WGSN survey data: 62%
of Gen Z and 63% of Millennials say their zodiac sign accurately reflects their
personality, and many use astrology to inform dating, career, and financial decisions.
The app Co-Star (launched 2017) has been downloaded 26M+ times and is credited with
lowering the entry cost to a system that was previously accessible mainly to people who
could pay a personal astrologer. The piece frames the resurgence as a response to
stress, uncertainty, and collective trauma (explicitly citing the pandemic), and as a
social lubricant — astrology gives people a low-stakes script for having vulnerable
conversations about identity and feelings they otherwise avoid. It also carries a
skeptical counter-voice: writer Haley Nahman calls the mainstreaming of astrology
possible evidence of "broader intellectual apathy" or a "scammy, pseudo-existential
branding exercise," while astrologer Emma Vidgen defends it as real spiritual practice
increasingly diluted by brands chasing the trend ("woo-woo washing").

## Key Points

- Mystical services market: ~$2.2B globally (2017–2023 estimate), astrology as its own
  category is a large and growing subset.
- 62% of Gen Z / 63% of Millennials self-report their zodiac sign accurately describes
  their personality (WGSN survey).
- Co-Star's technological lowering of the cost-of-entry to complex, personalized
  astrology (previously gated by access to a paid personal astrologer) is presented as
  the key mechanism of the resurgence, not the astrology content itself.
- Astrology functions as a conversational on-ramp to vulnerability — "a jumping off
  point for going deeper with whoever is in front of you" — filling a role that dense
  in-person social infrastructure used to fill.
- Two-sided read: astrologer Emma Vidgen frames the trend as democratized spirituality;
  writer Haley Nahman frames it as commodified pseudo-spirituality ("woo-woo washing")
  that brands exploit because it is currently on-trend.

## My Take

The mechanism this article isolates — technology (Co-Star) lowering the cost of entry
to a previously expert-gated, personalized interpretive system — is the same mechanism
by which LLMs are currently lowering the cost of entry to *every* previously
expert-gated interpretive service: therapy-adjacent reflection, legal reading, medical
triage, tutoring. Astrology's mass adoption is a small-scale, low-stakes precedent for
what happens when a personalized-guidance product becomes free and infinitely
available: usage explodes, use ranges from playful to load-bearing, and a skeptical
minority correctly worries that the volume of engagement outpaces anyone's ability to
verify the guidance is sound (Nahman's "intellectual apathy" critique reads almost
identically to current LLM-overreliance critiques). The dating/career/financial
decision-making use noted here is the concerning edge — the same edge case that shows
up when people use chatbots for decisions that exceed the tool's actual epistemic
authority.
