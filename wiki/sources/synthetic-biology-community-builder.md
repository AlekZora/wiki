## Summary

A profile/interview with David Sun Kong, MIT Media Lab professor and director of the Community Biotechnology Initiative. Kong's project is to democratize synthetic biology—not just by lowering tool costs (a $10 DIY DNA electroporator built from a flyswatter) but by building a global movement with explicit values, ethics, and shared purpose. He argues that the human community context of science is as important as the technical tools, and that synthetic biology's future should center nature and ecosystems rather than human commercial interests.

## Key Points

- Kong directs MIT Media Lab's Community Biotechnology Initiative and founded the Global Community Bio Summit, which has convened for 5+ years to build a worldwide network of community biolabs (300+ estimated globally as of 2019).
- Open-source tool development: a student built a DNA electroporator for ~$10 using a flyswatter as a voltage source. Metafluidics is an open-source repository for fluidics device designs, decoupling lab equipment from Western supply chains.
- "How to Grow (Almost) Anything" — MIT course taught with George Church that takes students with zero prior experience through synthetic biology from protein design to ecosystem engineering.
- Movement-building is central: Kong brought in Marshall Gantz (legendary community organizer; worked with MLK Jr. and Obama campaigns) to help the Bio Summit develop a shared purpose statement.
- Ethical emphasis: Kong argues ethics is underemphasized in graduate science education, and the community context (values, worldview, norms) shapes what technologies get built and how they're used.
- "Ancient Future Technology" course explicitly incorporates indigenous worldviews into science.
- Vision for synthetic biology: move from human-centric/commercial focus toward tools that benefit ecosystems and establish a mutualistic relationship with nature (e.g., reviving passenger pigeons and American chestnut trees via Revive and Restore).
- Biota Beats: a "microbial record player" that translates microbiome composition into music—an example of using biology as an artistic vehicle for public engagement.

## Quotes

> "A considerable part of my work is about expanding that participation and agency to as many diverse communities as possible worldwide, particularly communities that aren't traditionally part of the decision-making process."

> "I'm an academic, and ethics is often a very low priority. I think it should be emphasized much more in graduate school."

> "It's not science happening in a vacuum, in an arbitrary context. In our case, it's science occurring in the context of people trying to change the world for the better."

## My Take

The flyswatter electroporator is a perfect emblem of what Kong is doing: radical cost reduction that breaks dependency on Western supply chains, enabling participation from communities that were structurally excluded. But more interesting is his insistence that tools alone aren't enough—you need a community contract, shared ethics, and a movement. The connection to Hamming ("You and Your Research") and constructionism (Papert) is notable: learning by doing, in community, with real stakes.
