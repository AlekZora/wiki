## Summary

George Polya's slim, extraordinarily influential manual on mathematical problem-solving. Written as a guide for teachers and students, it distills the process of mathematical thinking into a four-phase method and a "dictionary of heuristic" — a catalogue of mental moves that good problem-solvers use. The book has had outsized influence far beyond mathematics, becoming a reference point in education, AI (early heuristic search), and general reasoning.

## Key Ideas

- **The four phases**: Polya's method: (1) Understand the problem — what is the unknown, what are the conditions? (2) Devise a plan — find a connection between the unknown and what you know. (3) Carry out the plan — execute and check each step. (4) Look back — examine the result, could you have done it differently?
- **Heuristic as discipline**: Polya treats problem-solving moves as nameable, teachable things: "Can you find a related problem?", "Can you restate the problem?", "Have you used all the data?", "Work backwards." These aren't tricks — they're a vocabulary for reasoning under uncertainty.
- **The teacher's role**: Good math teaching isn't demonstrating solutions — it's asking the right questions at the right moment so students generate the insight themselves. Telling someone the answer destroys the learning; guiding them to find it builds capability.
- **Analogy and generalization**: Two of the most powerful problem-solving moves are: find an analogous, simpler problem you can solve, then transfer the approach; and generalize beyond the specific problem to see if a broader principle is at work.
- **"Looking back" as underrated**: Most students solve a problem and move on. Polya insists the "look back" phase — checking the result, asking if there's a simpler proof, asking what the result generalizes to — is where real mathematical maturity develops.
- **Auxiliary elements**: Introducing new objects (a construction line, a new variable, an auxiliary equation) to create a connection between the known and unknown is one of the central creative moves in problem-solving.

## Quotes

> "The first rule of teaching is to know what you are supposed to teach. The second rule: know a little more than what you are supposed to teach."

> "The aim of the teacher should be to help the student. If he gives too much, he helps too much; if he gives too little, he helps too little."

## My Take

Polya's framework is useful far outside mathematics. "Devise a plan" is where most people fail — not because they can't execute, but because they grab the first plan that comes to mind rather than exploring the space of approaches. The heuristics section is a genuinely useful checklist: when stuck, try "can I solve a related but simpler problem?", "can I work backwards?", "have I used all the given information?" The teaching philosophy is essentially constructionist — understanding comes from generating, not receiving.
