## Summary

A synthesis piece (structured as a literature review with numbered citations) arguing
that single-player/console games and multiplayer/online games spread through
structurally different mechanisms, even though both ultimately depend on social
networks. Multiplayer games exhibit direct network effects — the game becomes more
valuable as more of a player's friends join, because other players are functionally
part of the product (opponents, teammates, matchmaking pools). Single-player/console
games instead depend on indirect, communication-driven word-of-mouth: a 2025 BMC
Psychology study models a three-phase "user snowball effect" — acceptance (WOM shapes
pre-purchase expectations), resonance (actual play confirms or disconfirms those
expectations, generating attractiveness/pleasure/value), and communication (satisfied
players share their experience, restarting the cycle). Multiplayer diffusion instead
runs through social identity and prosocial behavior formed *during* play, which
directly predicts positive WOM and direct recruitment ("play with me"), and exhibits a
critical-mass tipping point once a friend group's adoption reaches a threshold where
availability of partners makes the game meaningfully more attractive. The piece
synthesizes a structural comparison table (network-effect type, primary diffusion
mechanism, role of other players, psychological drivers, social-graph pattern,
importance of exposure, and failure mode) and notes hybrid designs — asynchronous
social traces layered onto single-player cores — as an emerging middle ground.

## Key Points

- Multiplayer = direct network effects (value scales with friend adoption);
  single-player/console = indirect network effects mediated by communication (WOM).
- Console/single-player WOM snowball model: acceptance → resonance → communication,
  each phase mediated by game attractiveness, pleasure arousal, and value perception;
  amplified by exposure (ads, storefront visibility).
- Multiplayer WOM is driven by social identity and prosocial behavior formed through
  co-play, which is a different causal chain from single-player WOM (expectation
  confirmation) even though both terminate in "recommend to a friend."
- Multiplayer games hit critical-mass tipping points (adoption threshold within a
  friend group after which growth accelerates); single-player games have no equivalent
  threshold because utility doesn't depend on friends' adoption.
- Failure modes differ: single-player games can be critically acclaimed but never
  generate enough early WOM to start the snowball; multiplayer games can acquire users
  but fail to cross the network-effect threshold, causing matchmaking/social collapse.
- Hybrid designs (asynchronous co-presence, ghost data, shared persistent worlds
  layered on a single-player spine) can capture both mechanisms at once.

## My Take

This maps cleanly onto multi-agent AI adoption dynamics. A single-agent AI tool is
"single-player" in this framework — its growth depends on a WOM snowball of
expectation-confirmation-sharing, and it can be excellent yet fail to spread if the
early resonance phase (does the tool actually deliver the promised value on first
use) doesn't land. A multi-agent or collaborative AI product (shared workspaces,
agents that coordinate with a user's team) is "multiplayer" in this framework —
its value is functionally tied to how many collaborators also adopt it, so it needs
to cross a critical-mass threshold within a team or org before it becomes
self-sustaining, and its growth mechanism is social-identity-driven recruitment
("get the rest of your team on this") rather than pure word-of-mouth. This suggests
different growth strategies are structurally required depending on which category a
given AI product falls into, and that treating a fundamentally single-player AI tool
with multiplayer-style growth tactics (or vice versa) would misfire for the same
reason it misfires in games.
