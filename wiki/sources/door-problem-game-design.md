## Summary

Liz England explains game design to outsiders using the humble door as a case study. A door in a game involves a cascade of decisions spanning every discipline — art, engineering, AI, audio, UI, production, QA, and more. The "Door Problem" illustrates both what a game designer actually does (resolves the interaction design of mundane elements) and how the work of a full studio is distributed across roles.

## Key Points

- A game designer's job is not the flashy creative stuff — it's solving practical interaction problems that nobody else owns. Doors are the canonical example.
- The full question tree: Can players open doors? Which ones? How do they know the difference? What if co-op players split? What if enemies need to fit through? What happens mid-streaming level load?
- Every role in the studio relates to doors differently: Creative Director approves them, Concept Artist paints them, Composer writes a theme for them, Monetization Designer charges $0.99 to open them now, QA Tester dies and reloads and walks to them.
- The mundanity is the point: game design is not about having wild ideas, it's about solving the practical questions that real interactive systems create.

## Quotes

> "SOMEONE has to solve The Door Problem, and that someone is a designer."

> "There's an impression that game design is flashy and cool and about crazy ideas and fun all the time. But when I start off with, 'Let me tell you about doors…' it cuts straight to the everyday practical considerations."

> "Player: I totally didn't even notice a door there."

## My Take

A brilliant pedagogical frame. The Player's line at the end is the punchline and the real lesson: great design is invisible. The discipline breakdown is useful for anyone trying to understand game studio structure. The Door Problem is also a meta-point about design work in general — the gap between the work and the perception of the work is enormous.
