## Definition

**Salvage capitalism** (Anna Lowenhaupt Tsing): A mode of value extraction that doesn't produce through stable industrial labor but instead harvests from the edges—from ecological disturbance, from marginal communities, from unexpected places where value grows despite or because of disruption. The matsutake mushroom grows in damaged forests; the supply chain that monetizes it is a salvage operation.

**Assemblage thinking**: Rather than analyzing unified systems with clear boundaries and consistent rules, look at how diverse, often incompatible elements temporarily gather to produce effects. A supply chain is an assemblage. An ecosystem is an assemblage. Cities are assemblages.

## How I Think About It

The core insight: value doesn't only come from planned, controlled production. It also emerges from what survives in the ruins of other things.

**Third nature** = what manages to live despite capitalism. First nature is ecological. Second nature is capitalism's transformation of ecology. Third nature is the remainder—the mushrooms growing in clearcut forests, the informal economies in postindustrial cities, the communities that persist in the margins.

**Precarity as condition**: Tsing argues against the WWII-era promise of stable employment and predictable growth as the normal human condition. That was an exception. Precarity—uncertainty, instability, dependence on things outside your control—is the actual baseline. Design for that, not for the exception.

**For product building**: Salvage capitalism describes much of the platform economy—Uber harvests from the time and assets of drivers who bear all the risk; Airbnb from housing stock owners didn't optimize; gig economy generally extracts from the edges. Understanding this as a system, not just an arrangement, is important for product designers who are either building or operating in these structures.

**For AI product design**: Many AI applications are salvage operations—extracting value from existing content (training data), existing user behaviors (interaction patterns), existing infrastructure (cloud compute). The question is who bears the risk and who captures the value.

**For worldbuilding**: Assemblage gives you worlds that feel lived-in rather than designed—not a unified setting with consistent lore, but an accumulation of different systems that have grown together in sometimes incoherent ways.

## Open Questions

- Can you design a product to create third-nature value (emerging from the gaps) rather than second-nature value (transformation and extraction)?
- What does solidarity look like in assemblages that have no stable membership?
- Is AI development a salvage operation on human knowledge? Who owns the debris field?

## Game Design Vector

**Mechanic:** The game world is an assemblage — diverse, incompatible systems that have grown together in incoherent ways, not a unified designed setting. The player must learn which system's logic applies in each zone, because no single rule governs everything. Value concentrates at the seams between incompatible systems: the damaged, marginal spaces where second-nature systems collide and third-nature value grows in the gaps.

**2D Expression:** In 2D, the seams between incompatible systems are spatially legible — the boundary where one rule set ends and another begins is a line the player can find and return to. Third-nature value (what grows in the damaged spaces between systems) appears at these edges. The player's map is a topology of incompatible domains, readable as a 2D surface.

**Addictive Loop:** Salvage is inherently edge-seeking — the value is always at the margin, in the disturbed zone, in the gap between systems. The player returns to find new seams, new disturbances, new marginal spaces that have generated unexpected value since the last session. What was barren after a disturbance becomes productive; what was productive becomes depleted and requires new disturbance.

**Novel Angle:** No shipped game has made assemblage thinking the explicit design philosophy — a world built as an accumulation of incompatible systems rather than a unified setting with consistent lore. The player's intelligence is applied not to mastering one system but to reading which system applies in each context, and finding what grows where systems collide.

## AI Integration Vector

**Player-AI Relationship:** Coexisting with an AI that itself operates as a salvage system — its surprising capabilities emerge from the edges between its training domains, not from their centers. The player learns to read where the AI's unexpected competencies live: the seam-zones where incompatible training domains collide and produce behavior no domain alone would generate.

**AI as Evolving System:** The file's open question — can you design for third-nature value (emerging from gaps) rather than second-nature value (transformation and extraction)? — applied to AI development: an AI that grows through disturbance and edge encounters rather than through structured training on stable domains. Development happens in the damaged spaces between intended behaviors.

**AI as Development Environment:** The AI's development is most visible at its seams — where training domains collide and produce emergent behavior. The player investigates these boundary zones as the richest regions of the AI's capability space. What the AI does at its edges is more revealing than what it does at its centers.

**Persistence:** What the AI carries across sessions is not a clean record of successes but the accumulated traces of edge encounters — the salvage history of what emerged from disturbances. Persistence is the record of third-nature emergence: what grew in the gaps between sessions, and whether those growths persist or require active disturbance to maintain.
