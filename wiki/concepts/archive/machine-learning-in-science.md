## Definition

The application of machine learning—particularly neural networks and deep learning—to scientific research problems, including anomaly detection, signal classification, data reduction, and generative modeling. Over the last decade ML has shifted from an optional add-on to a core method in particle physics, astrophysics, and increasingly all quantitative sciences.

## How I Think About It

ML in science sits at an interesting intersection: it's not just a better tool, it enables qualitatively different research. Anomaly detection at the LHC—searching for signatures of unknown new physics without a pre-specified model to search for—would be nearly impossible without ML. This is the "model-agnostic search" paradigm: let the data tell you something is unusual, then investigate. That's a fundamental shift in how science is done.

The education gap is also interesting. Physics has always required students to work with data, but the gap between coursework and modern practice grew dramatically over 10 years. Students were arriving at labs knowing equations but not knowing how to handle the actual computational pipeline. The solution is still evolving—ML education in physics is being retrofitted into curricula that weren't designed for it.

There's a deeper point about timelines: physics analyses that take 6 years hurt science structurally, because graduate students can burn their entire PhD on a slow analysis. ML compression of timelines isn't just efficiency—it changes what careers are viable in physics.
