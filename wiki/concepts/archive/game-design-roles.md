## Definition

The distribution of creative and technical responsibilities across the disciplines of a game studio. Game *design* specifically is the role responsible for resolving interaction design questions — the rules, systems, and player experience decisions that nobody else owns.

## How I Think About It

Liz England's Door Problem is the canonical illustration: a door in a game is not the concern of any one role, but someone has to answer all the hard questions about it — and that someone is the designer. The designer doesn't make the art, program the behavior, or record the audio, but they decide what the door should do, how a player understands it, and what happens in every edge case.

The discipline breakdown from the Door Problem maps onto two things:
1. **What each role actually does** — creative directors approve, concept artists envision, environment artists build, animators move, QA breaks.
2. **How invisible good design is** — the final line is the Player saying "I totally didn't even notice a door there," which is the point. Successful interaction design erases itself.

From the Corgispace article, Adam Saltsman's *idea vs. formula* distinction adds texture: game designers are most valuable when they're working from live ideas, not calcified formulas. A door design that comes from a formula ("all doors are green when open, red when locked") may solve the functional problem but miss the experiential opportunity.

Lemarchand pushes this further: "every member of a development team is a game designer," because their moment-to-moment decisions collectively shape the player experience. The designer's unique role isn't gatekeeping design — it's holding the [Experience Goals](experience-goals.md) and ensuring all decisions across disciplines serve them. The Door Problem and this claim are complementary: someone must own the door questions, but everyone making the door is shaping the experience.
