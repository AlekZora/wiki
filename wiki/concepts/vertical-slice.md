## Definition

A polished, shippable-quality demo of a representative section of the final product. Unlike a horizontal prototype (which shows breadth at low quality), a vertical slice proves depth: final-quality art, sound, mechanics, and feel for a narrow portion of the experience. Its purpose is to validate that the target experience is achievable and to generate realistic production cost data.

## How I Think About It

The vertical slice answers the question every creative project eventually faces: "Can we actually make this thing feel the way we imagined?" A concept doc can't answer it. A grey-box prototype can't answer it. Only a finished-quality fragment can.

Lemarchand traces this to Mark Cerny's "Method" (Crash Bandicoot, Jak & Daxter): preproduction isn't done until you have a "publishable first playable." The word "publishable" is key — not a tech demo, not a proof of concept, but something you'd be willing to ship if the whole game were at that quality.

The vertical slice also forces honesty about scope. If one level takes 3 months to bring to shippable quality, and you have 12 months of production, you have roughly 4 levels — not the 20 you imagined. Teams that skip the slice discover this too late and crunch.

The risk: teams can over-polish the slice and produce something unsustainably expensive. The slice should be representative of sustainable production quality, not a one-off showpiece.

## Project Connection

For Dead Reckoning: before building out a full season, produce one fully realized episode — final-quality animation, voice, character interaction, reveal structure. This single episode proves whether the core mechanic (hidden agendas surfacing through emergent interaction) actually produces compelling daily content. If it doesn't work at episode scale, scale decisions are premature.
