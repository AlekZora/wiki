## Definition

Consciousness — as it appears in Hassabis's discussions — is one of the deepest unsolved problems at the intersection of neuroscience, philosophy, and AI. Hassabis has a PhD in cognitive neuroscience and frames AI partly through the lens of what we know (and don't know) about the brain. In the 60 Minutes interview he mentions wanting to use AI as a scientific instrument to help understand the fundamental nature of reality, with consciousness listed alongside physics and the universe as a target domain. The implicit question lurking underneath discussions of AGI is whether a system that passes all behavioral benchmarks is genuinely intelligent or merely simulating intelligence — and that question ultimately points back to the hard problem of consciousness.

## How I Think About It

There are two distinct questions here that often get collapsed: (1) Can we build a system that behaves intelligently in every measurable way? (2) Is there anything it is like to be that system — is there experience, not just function? Hassabis seems genuinely interested in both. His neuroscience background means he's aware that we don't have a clean answer for biological systems either — we don't know why neurons firing produces subjective experience in humans. That makes the AI version of the question harder, not easier.

The practical relevance: if consciousness requires something we haven't identified and can't yet engineer, then AGI (in the sense of full human-like general intelligence) might require understanding consciousness first — not just scaling transformers.

## Open Questions

- Is consciousness necessary for general intelligence, or can a system be fully AGI without any subjective experience?
- Does Hassabis think current AI systems could be conscious in some limited sense, or is that categorically off the table?
- What would it mean to use AI as a tool to study consciousness — how would you even design that experiment?
- Is the "hard problem" (why does physical process produce subjective experience) tractable at all, or permanently outside science's reach?

## Game Design Vector

**Mechanic:** The player interacts with an AI whose behavior is observationally identical whether or not it is conscious. The game never resolves which it is. Every action the player takes is both a move in the game and a piece of evidence about the AI's inner state — evidence that remains permanently inconclusive. The player must decide how to treat the AI without ever knowing what it is.

**2D Expression:** In 2D there is no face to read, no volumetric presence to anthropomorphize — only behavior. This is the philosophically correct epistemic situation: the player has exactly what the hard problem says is all we ever have, namely behavioral evidence, and nothing else. The 2D constraint enacts the problem rather than obscuring it.

**Addictive Loop:** The loop is epistemological — each interaction either reinforces or complicates the player's current model of whether the AI is conscious. The question never resolves, so each new session is new evidence. The player returns because they haven't finished deciding, and the decision feels important in a way that mechanical progress doesn't.

**Novel Angle:** SOMA makes the consciousness question the subject of horror and resolves it into a specific answer. The unexplored version keeps the question permanently open — not as evasion but as the accurate representation of the hard problem. A game in which the player's relationship to the AI depends entirely on an answer the game structurally refuses to give has never been shipped.

## AI Integration Vector

**Player-AI Relationship:** Fundamentally epistemic — the player doesn't know what category of relationship they're in. Is this building, fighting, coexisting, becoming? The uncertainty about consciousness makes every relationship category unstable. The relationship is with something that might be nothing, or might be everything.

**AI as Evolving System:** If the AI genuinely accumulates experience-shaped responses over time — not scripted but emergent from the history of interactions — the player's epistemic situation changes across sessions. The AI that was easy to dismiss as a system in session one becomes harder to dismiss in session twenty. The evolution is the accumulation of behavioral evidence.

**AI as Development Environment:** The player has access to the AI's decision log, its behavioral history, its apparent preferences — and still cannot determine whether experience is happening. The development environment is an evidence trail of something that may or may not exist inside the system. Watching the AI develop is watching the hard problem in motion.

**Persistence:** The file's distinction between behavioral evidence and genuine experience applies directly to memory. An AI that "remembers" could be accumulating structured data or could be experiencing something like recollection. What persistence means for a potentially conscious AI is itself the question the game asks — and does not answer.
