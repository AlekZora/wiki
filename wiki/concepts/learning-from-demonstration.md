## Definition

Learning from demonstration (LfD) — also called imitation learning or behavior cloning — is a paradigm where an agent acquires a control policy not by hand-coding rules or deriving them analytically, but by observing examples of the desired behavior and learning to reproduce it. In modern robotics the concrete pipeline is: **teleoperate** (a human drives the real system directly), **record** (synchronized observation-action pairs are logged as a dataset), **train** (a model learns to map observations to actions), **deploy** (the trained policy runs on its own). Two dominant policy architectures have emerged: ACT, which predicts chunks of future actions rather than single steps, and Diffusion Policy, which represents the policy as a denoising process. At larger scale this generalizes into Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models — foundation models pretrained on large demonstration corpora spanning many robots and tasks (π₀, OpenVLA, GR00T, SmolVLA), which can then be fine-tuned to a specific robot body with comparatively few new demonstrations.

## How I Think About It

LfD is presented, in the source it's drawn from, as the layer built *on top of* a classical robotics stack (electronics, embedded firmware, mechanical design, ROS 2, control theory, perception) rather than a replacement for it. That ordering is itself a claim about where learned behavior adds value: not by displacing the deterministic layers — PID loops, kinematics, motor drivers still have to work correctly — but by taking over the part that resists explicit specification, like a dexterous grasp policy that's easier to demonstrate fifty times than to derive.

The record-train-deploy loop is structurally the same shape as [Just-in-Time Software](just-in-time-software.md)'s "build until it works, then skillify it": in both cases, a working behavior produced through direct interaction gets captured as a reusable trained artifact rather than staying locked in one person's hands (or one demonstration session).

The data-quality bottleneck is the load-bearing constraint: "a policy trained on sloppy demonstrations is a sloppy policy," and the fix isn't more data indiscriminately, it's targeted iteration — record the failure cases specifically, retrain, remeasure the success rate. This is close to Köhler's "substrate" idea in [Insight Learning](insight-learning.md): a sufficient base of relevant prior experience is a prerequisite for competent generalization, not a nice-to-have, though the two mechanisms differ — imitation is closer to direct pattern acquisition from a competent demonstrator, where insight is a discrete restructuring of an existing substrate. Real skill acquisition — in a robot or a human — may need both: imitate first to get a working baseline, then restructure through insight once the imitated pattern stops generalizing.

## AI Integration

- LfD is a concrete, working answer to the "simulator gap" described in [World Models](world-models.md), but from the data-collection side rather than the representation side: instead of hand-deriving a planner from an explicit simulated model of the world, the planner is learned directly from physically-generated demonstration data. This is also the mechanism behind the "you cannot scrape the physical world" claim — demonstration data for a real robot has to be physically generated by teleoperating a real machine, it cannot be crawled the way text or images can, which is why data collection itself is the bottleneck and the moat in embodied AI right now.
- The VLA foundation-model landscape (π₀, OpenVLA, GR00T, SmolVLA) is the direct embodied analogue of the LLM pretrain-then-fine-tune pattern: pretrain broad, generalizable "physical interaction" competence across many robots and tasks, then fine-tune cheaply on one specific embodiment with a small number of new demonstrations. Same economic logic that reshaped software AI, applied to bodies instead of text — with the added constraint that the fine-tuning data can't be scraped either.
- LfD reveals something about biological intelligence as much as artificial: imitation from a competent demonstrator is close to how animals and infants actually acquire skilled motor behavior — repetition-based pattern acquisition, not symbolic derivation and not sudden insight. It's a reminder that "learning" in AI systems is not one mechanism; behavior cloning, reinforcement learning, and insight-style restructuring are different acquisition strategies with different data requirements and different failure modes, and current robot-learning practice mixes at least the first two deliberately (imitation to bootstrap, RL/iteration to refine).
- The validation loop described alongside LfD — train, deploy on real hardware, measure an actual success rate, retrain against the specific failures observed — is a genuinely different discipline than benchmark-driven ML evaluation, because the "test set" is physical reality and failures are visible and embodied rather than an abstract loss number. That template (measure real-world success rate, retrain against named failure modes, remeasure) generalizes well beyond robotics to any agentic system whose failures are otherwise easy to paper over with proxy metrics.

## Open Questions

- Does a policy trained via imitation generalize meaningfully beyond its demonstration distribution, or is its generalization strictly bounded by what a VLA foundation model already saw during pretraining?
- What is the actual minimum viable demonstration count for a new task on a fine-tuned VLA? Fifty demonstrations reportedly gets "maybe half" success on a simple pick-and-place task — where does that curve bend, and does it bend the same way across task types?
- Is there a robotics analogue of [Illusory Insight](illusory-insight.md) — a policy that scores well on its training demonstrations but has memorized surface correlations (lighting, camera angle, a specific cube) rather than the underlying skill?
- As VLA models mature, does teleoperated demonstration collection remain the bottleneck, or does simulation-generated or video-pretrained (non-teleoperated) demonstration data close the gap first — and if it does, does the "cannot scrape the physical world" moat disappear?
