## Short Answer

Yes, but it requires a specific structural technique: the writer must never establish the counterfactual baseline. If the audience never clearly sees what the timeline looked like before the intervention, they cannot determine whether the intervention changed it. Sustained model ambiguity is an epistemic effect, not a plotting trick.

---

## Why It's Hard

The three temporal models make different observable predictions:

- **Immutable**: attempts to change always fail or turn out to have been what caused the original events
- **Mutable**: at least some attempted interventions visibly succeed in altering what follows
- **Many-worlds**: a fork is created; the traveler is now in a demonstrably different branch

Any evidence that distinguishes between these collapses the ambiguity. The usual failure mode is that screenwriters introduce a "before" state — something the traveler saw before they traveled — and then show whether that state matches the post-intervention world. The moment you can compare before and after, you have your answer.

---

## The Required Technique: No Counterfactual Baseline

For model ambiguity to hold, the audience must not know what would have happened without the intervention. This means:

1. **The protagonist cannot have strong memories of the original timeline** that can be checked against outcomes. If they remember clearly what "should" have happened and then see that it did happen anyway, you have confirmed immutability. If they see it diverged, you have confirmed mutability.

2. **Attempted interventions must have ambiguous causal signatures.** Did the protagonist cause X, or was X going to happen regardless? The story must construct events where the protagonist's contribution is genuinely underdetermined. This is only possible when the effect could have occurred through multiple independent causal paths — some involving the intervention, some not.

3. **The ending must reward both readings.** The final state of the story must be interpretable as: (a) exactly what was always going to happen, or (b) the result of genuine change. Stories that close with a neat resolution typically announce the model in the resolution's logic.

---

## Cases Where It Works

**Primer (2004)** — achieves this, largely accidentally, through deliberate opacity. The timeline becomes so tangled that audience members leave with genuinely different models of what happened. It works through information overload rather than dual-interpretation construction, but the effect is real. Different viewings produce different causal maps.

**Terminator (1984)** — has an unresolved model question embedded in its premise. John Connor exists because Kyle Reese went back and fathered him. Is this immutable (always was going to happen — the loop was always closed) or mutable (Kyle's intervention created John, meaning John's existence depended on a contingent decision)? The film never resolves this. Both readings are internally consistent.

**Arrival (2016)** — not traditional time travel, but the heptapod model of time is ambiguous in exactly this way until the final sequence. The audience does not know whether Louise is remembering, imagining, or perceiving across time — and whether her knowledge of what is coming means she can change it or is experiencing its inevitability.

---

## The Structural Requirement

Sustained model ambiguity requires that the protagonist's causal contribution to key events be *dually interpretable* at the story's end:

> *This would have happened anyway, and the protagonist's actions were part of what always caused it.*
> — AND —
> *This happened because the protagonist intervened, and without that intervention it would not have.*

Both must be defensible from the same evidence. This is genuinely constructable, but it demands that the writer resist the natural instinct to prove the story's internal logic by clarifying who caused what.

---

## The Dramatic Function

Sustaining this ambiguity puts the audience in the same epistemic position as a character who cannot know if their choices matter. That is the thematic payload — not a puzzle, but an experience. The audience doesn't know if they're watching fate or will. The discomfort of not resolving the question is the point.

This is a different use than the standard time travel story, which typically uses model revelation as a climax (the moment the protagonist realizes they're in a predestination paradox, or that they've successfully changed things). Sustained ambiguity sacrifices that climax in exchange for an ongoing phenomenological state: the audience lives in uncertainty rather than being released from it.

---

## Practical Limit

Pure sustained ambiguity is difficult to maintain without the story feeling evasive or unearned. The workable version is **late disambiguation with adequate prior ambiguity** — the model is not announced but becomes clear in retrospect, in a way that reframes what came before. *Dark* (Season 1) does this: the immutability of the timeline is confirmed fairly late, and the prior seasons hold the question open long enough that the reveal lands as revelation rather than rule-clarification.

Full end-to-end ambiguity (model never revealed, story over, both readings valid) is rare because it requires an ending that doesn't inadvertently adjudicate the question. Most endings imply an answer through their emotional register: catharsis implies mutability (something was genuinely achieved), tragedy implies immutability (nothing could have been changed). Sustaining the ambiguity requires an ending that is neither — or that works as both simultaneously.
