## Definition

When access to an experience, place, or moment becomes easy or repeatable, it loses its weight. The tourist is present but not committed — they can leave, undo, revisit. The authentic participant cannot. The tourist problem is the principle that **accessibility and stakes exist in inverse proportion**: the more freely something can be undone or revisited, the less any single instance of it matters.

The name comes from time travel fiction, where history stripped of permanence becomes a destination rather than a foundation — tourists watching the Titanic sink, the crucifixion, the fall of Rome, present but untouchable, unable to commit because they can always leave.

## How I Think About It

The tourist problem is the stakes problem in disguise. Stakes don't come from consequences being large — they come from consequences being *irreversible*. You can't be a tourist if you can't leave.

This is why permadeath in games feels categorically different from respawning, even when the mechanical experience of dying is identical. The difference isn't punishment — it's ontological. With permadeath, this exact run, this exact configuration of choices and encounters, will never exist again. Without it, you're a tourist to your own playthrough. You can come back. So it doesn't count.

The tourist problem explains a phenomenon that's easy to mistake for poor writing or poor design: why do players or readers stop caring about a world that seems to have everything? Because when nothing is final, everything is optional. Optional things feel like tourism.

The inverse of the tourist problem: **singularity produces presence**. The moment that cannot be revisited — the NPC that dies permanently, the conversation that forecloses a path, the world that changes and doesn't change back — is the moment that demands the player actually be there. Singularity is what makes presence possible.

A subtlety worth holding: the tourist problem doesn't require the player to *use* the undo mechanic. Even knowing it's available dissolves stakes. The save state doesn't have to be loaded — just knowing you could is enough to make you a tourist.

## Design Applications

**Game design / mechanics:**
- Permadeath as the design choice that eliminates the tourist problem entirely — not because it punishes failure but because it makes each run singular
- The save-scum problem: even in games without explicit save scumming, any mechanic that allows a player to reverse a decision (reloadable saves, rewind systems, undo buttons) must be evaluated against whether it converts authentic participants into tourists
- Intentional tourist mechanics: there are games where the player is *meant* to feel like a tourist — a visitor to a history that doesn't need them, an observer without agency. *Her Story*, some of *Disco Elysium*. The tourist feeling is the design goal, not a failure. Requires recognizing the problem explicitly so it can be chosen deliberately.
- Procedural generation as a partial solution: if every dungeon is uniquely generated and unrecoverable once left, the player is never a tourist — they can never go back to the same place. Each moment is inherently singular.
- Consequence systems that "remember" rather than "punish": the goal isn't to make failure costly but to make choices permanent. NPCs who remember what you did. World states that don't reset. Doors that close.

**Narrative / storytelling:**
- Death in fiction follows the tourist problem exactly: readers tolerate deaths of secondary characters because resurrection feels available; they mourn protagonists in worlds where death is final. Superhero comics have nearly eliminated stakes through the normalization of resurrection — everyone is a tourist now.
- The tutorial as tourist trap: tutorials that let players experiment without consequences produce tourists. The best onboarding teaches through consequence, not through a consequence-free sandbox.
- First-person narrators who survived are already tourists to their own past — they're telling you a story they lived through. The dramatic irony that the narrator exists (survived) subtly reduces stakes. Present-tense narration or unreliable narration can partially counteract this.
- Serialization vs. closed narrative: serialized stories (TV seasons, game sequels) that always return characters to baseline between entries create the tourist problem at franchise scale. The character is never permanently changed, so nothing that happens to them matters.

**Worldbuilding / systems:**
- Any civilization with functional time travel develops the tourist problem at civilizational scale: history is a destination, catastrophes are attractions, the Titanic has been watched sinking thousands of times by visitors who felt the experience without consequence. The ethical and psychological management systems required to prevent full nihilism would be the dominant institutions of that civilization.
- A world without the tourist problem: one where every visit changes the visited. Not just causally (you could wipe your footprints) but ontologically — the act of witnessing makes you a participant, and participants don't get to leave without carrying something. This is the design premise of several horror traditions: the thing you saw now knows you saw it.
- Resource scarcity as tourist-problem management: when a resource is genuinely limited (one key, one opportunity, one conversation before the character dies), the tourist problem is mechanically impossible. Design systems that make scarcity real rather than simulated.

**Filmmaking / media:**
- Streaming vs. theatrical as tourist-problem variation: the ability to pause, rewind, and re-watch makes film viewing partial tourism. The theatrical experience — no pause, communal, unrepeatable — is closer to genuine participation. Directors who understand this design for the experience of watching once, not for the experience of re-watching analytically.
- Documentary subjects who are still alive vs. historical subjects: the living subject could change their position, retract, sue, respond. The documentary is live. Historical subjects are fixed — the viewer is always already a tourist because nothing that happens during viewing affects the subject. Stakes in documentary come from the subject's current vulnerability, not their historical situation.
- The sequel problem: sequels always risk retroactively converting the original into tourism. If the stakes of the original — the deaths, the losses, the choices — are undone or made irrelevant by the sequel, the audience is revealed to have been tourists all along.

- [[temporal-taxonomy]] — the three time-travel rule systems (immutable / mutable / many-worlds) produce different tourist-problem intensities: immutable timelines prevent tourism entirely (you can visit but never change); mutable timelines create tourism risk (you can undo); many-worlds is the deepest tourism of all (you never even arrive in your own timeline)
- [[the-perspective-flip]] — the reference-frame flip is itself a tourist-problem solution: it doesn't undo events, it recontextualizes them, making the audience a participant in a new understanding rather than a tourist to the original reading

## Open Questions

- Is there a principled distinction between a *respawn* and a *retry*? Does framing alone affect whether a player feels like a tourist, or only mechanical structure?
- Speedrunning converts a singular narrative experience into a repeatable mechanical exercise — the runner becomes a tourist to the story while becoming a deep participant in the optimization problem. What does this say about which layer of a game generates authentic stakes?
- Does the tourist problem apply in reverse? If something is *so* inaccessible that it can never be experienced (a destroyed historical site, a dead language), does inaccessibility produce the opposite of stakes — not tourism, but irrelevance?
- Is parasocial engagement with real people (streamers, influencers) a form of the tourist problem? The audience participates without consequence, the streamer absorbs all the stakes.
- What is the *minimum* irreversibility required for stakes to register? Is a single meaningful permanent consequence enough to activate authentic participation, or must the whole system be irreversible?
