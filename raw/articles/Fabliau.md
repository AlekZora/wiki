A ***fabliau*** (French pronunciation: [\[fabljo\]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French "Help:IPA/French"); plural ***fabliaux***) is a comic, often anonymous tale written by [jongleurs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jongleur "Jongleur") and clerics in [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France "France") between c. 1150 and 1400. They are generally characterized by sexual and [scatological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scatological "Scatological") [obscenity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Obscenity "Obscenity"), and by a set of contrary attitudes generally critical or mocking of the church and nobility.[^1] While most fabliaux were anonymous, we do know some authors like [Jean Bodel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Bodel "Jean Bodel") or Guèrin, who wrote during the peak of the genre's popularity. Several of them were reworked by [Giovanni Boccaccio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Boccaccio "Giovanni Boccaccio") for the *[Decameron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decameron "Decameron")* and by [Geoffrey Chaucer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Chaucer "Geoffrey Chaucer") for *[The Canterbury Tales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Canterbury_Tales "The Canterbury Tales")*. Some 150 French *fabliaux* are extant, the number depending on how narrowly *fabliau* is defined. According to R. Howard Bloch, *fabliaux* are the first expression of [literary realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_realism "Literary realism") in Europe.[^2]

Some nineteenth-century scholars, most notably [Gaston Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaston_Paris "Gaston Paris"), argue that *fabliaux* originally came from the [Orient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orient "Orient") and were brought to the West by returning [crusaders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crusades "Crusades").[^3]

## History and definition of the genre

The *fabliau* is defined as a short narrative in (usually octosyllabic) verse, between 300 and 400 lines long,[^8] its content is often comic or satiric.[^9] In France, it flourished in the 12th and 13th centuries; in England, it was popular in the 14th century.[^9] *Fabliaux* are often compared to the later [short story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_story "Short story"); [Douglas Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douglas_Bush "Douglas Bush"), longtime professor at [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University "Harvard University"), called it "a short story broader than it is long." [^10] Even though serving as short bawdy poetry, Fabliaux reflected the social disorder during its time. The time period underwent many changes marked by political instability, religious disputes, crusade wars, and the decline of a feudal system. Through humor and explicit language fabliaux was a medieval entertainment that mocked the flaws of society.

The *fabliau* is remarkable in that it seems to have no direct literary predecessor in the West, but was brought from the East by returning crusaders in the 12th century. Fabliaux were most famous in the courts. Popular amongst knights, noblemen, lords, and ladies, a very aristocratic group of individuals. Those accompanying those in the courts would be entertainers. Troubadours, who were poets and musicians.[^11] They sang love songs in Occitan poetry that was influenced by Arabic and Hebrew poetry. Fabliaux started as courtly love, where poets would express overwhelming love for a married noblewoman; yet she would be unattainable.[^12] As knights and nobles began to merge into one class, knights began to examine moral issues that came with their role. They begin to value chivalry, celebrating the arms of war, yet also exploring aspects of love and courting women [^13] Chivalry tales and epic poetry morphed into romance literature. Starting as an oral tradition, but began to be written down. Courtly love spread from Southern France to Italy, Northern France, England, and Germany. The translations changed minnesinger (love singer) in Germany, and the trouvere sang in old French in France. Actual fabliaux derived from northern France poetry. Fabliaux provided a more realistic aspect of human life.

The closest literary genre is the [fable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fable "Fable"), as found in [Aesop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesop "Aesop") "and its eastern origins or parallels," but it is less moral and less *didactic* than the fable.[^14] The word is a northern French diminutive from *fable*.." [^15] In terms of morality it is suggested to be closer to the [novel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novel "Novel") than to the [parable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parable "Parable"): "the story is the first thing, the moral the second, and the latter is never suffered to interfere with the former." [^14] Still, according to Robert Lewis, "some two-thirds of the French *fabliaux* have an explicit moral attached to them." [^15]

The earliest known *fabliau* is the anonymous *[Richeut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richeut "Richeut")* [^16] (c. 1159–1175 [^17]); one of the earliest known writers of *fabliaux* is [Rutebeuf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rutebeuf "Rutebeuf"), "the prototype of the jongleur of medieval literature." [^18]

The genre has been quite influential: passages in longer medieval poems such as *[Le Roman de Renart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Roman_de_Renart "Le Roman de Renart")* as well as tales found in collections like [Giovanni Boccaccio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Boccaccio "Giovanni Boccaccio") 's *[Decameron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decameron "Decameron")* and [Geoffrey Chaucer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Chaucer "Geoffrey Chaucer") 's *[Canterbury Tales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canterbury_Tales "Canterbury Tales")* have their origin in one or several *fabliaux*. Additionally, the medieval church also found use for the *fabliau* form. Noting its popularity, the church turned to their own form of minstrelsy similar to the *fabliau* that espoused "worthy thoughts" rather than the "ribaldry" a more typical *fabliau* would couch its moral in.[^19]

When the *fabliau* gradually disappeared, at the beginning of the 16th century, it was replaced by the [prose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prose "Prose") short story, which was greatly influenced by its predecessor.[^20] Famous French writers such as [Molière](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moli%C3%A8re "Molière"), [Jean de La Fontaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_de_La_Fontaine "Jean de La Fontaine"), and [Voltaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltaire "Voltaire") owe much to the tradition of the *fabliau*.[^21]

### Cast of characters, audience

Typical *fabliaux* contain a vast array of characters, including [cuckolded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuckold "Cuckold") husbands, rapacious [clergy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clergy "Clergy"), and foolish [peasants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peasant "Peasant"), as well as beggars, connivers, thieves, and whores. The clergy, in particular, are a group who are often singled out for criticism.[^22] The enforcement of clerical celibacy at the Second Lateran Council of 1139 caused many lay people to think differently about their local priest's marriages.[^23] As a result, taboo characters such as the adultering priest in Guerin's “The Priest Who Peeked” and the priest who drowned after being caught with the wife of a knight in “The Fisherman from Pont-sur-Seine” are extremely humorous to 12th and 13th century audiences. The status of peasants also appears to vary, based on the audience for which the *fabliau* was being written. Poems that were presumably written for the [nobility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobility "Nobility") portray peasants (*vilains* in [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language "French language")) as stupid and vile, whereas those written for the lower classes often tell of peasants getting the better of the clergy.

The subversion of expected gender roles is also a common theme within fabliaux. For example, in the story “Berangier of the Long Ass” a woman tricks her cowardly husband by dressing as a knight and confronting him. He begs for forgiveness, not recognizing his wife, and, by her request, kisses her bottom to make amends. The irony here not only comes from his mistaking of female genitals as a “long ass”, but also that the woman has served justice to her husband by forcing him to submit to her and putting herself in a dominant role.

The audience for *fabliaux* is estimated differently by different critics. Joseph Bédier suggests a bourgeois audience, which sees itself reflected in the urban settings and lower-class types portrayed in *fabliaux*.[^24] On the other hand, Per Nykrog argues that *fabliaux* were directed towards a noble audience, and concludes that *fabliaux* were that they made have been the same audience consuming courtly literature.[^25] This is reflected by the fact that the status of peasants appears to vary, based on the audience for which the fabliau was being written. Poems that were presumably written for the nobility portray peasants (vilains in French) as stupid and vile, whereas those written for the lower classes often tell of quick-witted and clever peasants getting the better of the clergy. For example, in the well-known fabliau “The Butcher of Abbeville”, a butcher is invited into a priest's home to dine, and deceives him by stealing his livestock and sleeping with the maid.

### Subject matter

The subject matter is often sexual: *fabliaux* are concerned with the elements of love left out by poets who wrote in the more elevated genres such as [Ovid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ovid "Ovid"), who suggests in the *[Ars Amatoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ars_Amatoria "Ars Amatoria")* (II.704–5) that the Muse should not enter the room where the lovers are in bed; and [Chrétien de Troyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chr%C3%A9tien_de_Troyes "Chrétien de Troyes"), who maintains silence on the exact nature of the joy discovered by Lancelot and Guinevere in *[Le Chevalier de la Charrette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Chevalier_de_la_Charrette "Le Chevalier de la Charrette")* (4676–4684).[^26] *[Lais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lai_\(poetic_form\) "Lai (poetic form)")* and *fabliaux* have much in common; an example of a poem straddling the fence between the two genres is " [Lecheor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lecheor "Lecheor") ".

*Fabliaux* derive a lot of their force from puns and other verbal figures; "fabliaux... are obsessed with wordplay." Especially important are [paranomasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paranomasia "Paranomasia") and [catachresis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catachresis "Catachresis"), tropes which disrupt ordinary signification and displace ordinary meanings [^27] —by similarity of sound, for instance, one can have both "con" and "conte" ("cunt" and "tale") in the same word, a common pun in *fabliaux*.[^28]

Humor is a main purpose served by fabliaux, as mentioned in Joseph Bedier's 1925 definition of the genre as verse tales meant for laughter, “contes à rire en vers” [^29] This definition has been largely sufficient for many scholars of fabliaux, although there is some debate on whether entertainment is the main purpose of the stories, or rather an outlet through which to teach a lesson to the audience.

### Form

The standard form of the *fabliau* is that of [Medieval French literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_French_literature "Medieval French literature") in general, the [octosyllable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octosyllable "Octosyllable") [rhymed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhyme "Rhyme") [couplet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Couplet "Couplet"), the most common verse form used in verse [chronicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chronicle "Chronicle"), [romances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_\(heroic_literature\) "Romance (heroic literature)") (the *romans*), *[lais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lai_\(poetic_form\) "Lai (poetic form)")*, and *dits*. They are generally short, a few hundred lines; Douin de L'Avesne's *Trubert*, at 2984 lines, is exceptionally long.

### "Browny, the Priest's Cow"

![](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/db/Cow_from_BL_Harley_4751.jpg/250px-Cow_from_BL_Harley_4751.jpg)

Illustration of a cow from a 13th century manuscript

Browny, the Priest's Cow was written by Jean Bodel in the early 1200s.[^30] In the story, a farmer and his wife take a pastor's sermon very seriously, and offer their only cow to the church because they believe that God will return what they have given to him twofold. The pastor, deciding to take advantage of the peasant's gift, accepts and puts the cow in the pasture with his own cow, Browny. The two animals are tied together until the farmer's cow gets used to the boundaries of the field, until she becomes restless and decides to go home. Thanks to the lead, she takes Browny along with her. The Farmer and his wife are rewarded with the return of their cow and a second, fatter and more plentiful cow, while the priest is punished for his greed by losing his prized cow. It's heavily critical of the church and its tithing, something that became a staple of fabliaux.[^31]

### "Gombert et les deus clers"

A well-known storyline is found in "Gombert et les deus clers" ("Gombert and the two clerks"). It was also written by Jean Bodel in the 1200s, and was likely adapted from earlier folklore. Two traveling clerks (students) take up lodging with a *villain*, and share the bedroom with Gombert, his beautiful wife, and their two children—one teenage girl, and one baby. One of the clerks climbs into bed with the teenage daughter and, promising her his ring, has his way with her; the other, while Gombert is "ala pissier" ("gone pissing", 85), moves the crib with the baby so that Gombert, on his return, lies down in the bed occupied by the clerks—one of whom is in bed with his daughter, while the other is now having sex with Gombert's wife, who thinks it is Gombert come to pleasure her. When the first clerk returns to his bed where he thinks his friend still is, he tells Gombert all about his adventure: "je vien de fotre / mes que ce fu la fille a l'oste" ("I've just been fucking, and if it wasn't the host's daughter", 152–53). Gombert attacks the first clerk, but ends up being beaten up by both.[^32]

The tale is found practically unchanged in [Boccaccio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boccaccio "Boccaccio") 's *[Decameron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summary_of_Decameron_tales#Sixth_tale_.28IX.2C_6.29 "Summary of Decameron tales")* and in [Chaucer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Chaucer "Geoffrey Chaucer") 's " [The Reeve's Tale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Reeve%27s_Tale "The Reeve's Tale") ".

### "L'enfant de neige"

In "L'enfant de neige" (" [The snow baby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Snow-child "The Snow-child") "), a [black comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_comedy "Black comedy"), a merchant returns home after an absence of two years to find his wife with a newborn son. She explains one snowy day she swallowed a snowflake while thinking about her husband, which caused her to conceive. Pretending to believe the "miracle", they raise the boy until the age of 15 when the merchant takes him on a business trip to [Genoa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genoa "Genoa"). There, he sells the boy into [slavery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavery "Slavery"). On his return, he explains to his wife that the sun burns bright and hot in [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy "Italy"); since the boy was begotten by a snowflake, he melted in the heat.[^33]

### "Bérangier au lonc cul" (Bérangier of the long arse)

" [De Bérangier au lonc cul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_B%C3%A9rangier_au_lonc_cul "De Bérangier au lonc cul") " is a medieval French *fabliau*.[^34] There are two versions of the *fabliau*: one by Guèrin and one anonymous. In summary, the story begins when a rich earl marries his daughter off to a "young peasant" and deems him a knight. The knight abandons the code of chivalry and lazes around for the first ten years of the marriage. When his wife, tired of his demeaning attitude and lazy nature, speaks of the greatness of the knights in her family, the husband decides to prove himself a worthy knight. He dresses in armor and goes into the forest on horseback. Once in the forest, he hangs his shield on the lowest branch of a tree and beats it until it looks as if it endured a great battle. The knight returns to his wife, shows her his bruised armor, and gloats about his victories. After a few trips into the forest, the wife begins to wonder why the knight himself is unscathed while his armor is in shambles. The next day, she suggests he take servants with him. When he refuses, the lady dresses in a full body suit of armor and follows him into the forest. When she sees him beating his own shield, she steps into sight and threatens to kill him for his dishonor to chivalry. The knight does not recognize his wife's voice. He begs for "pity" and offers to do anything to avoid conflict. His wife, disguised as a mighty knight, gives him the option of jousting her, in which he will surely die, or kissing her arse. Out of cowardice, the knight chooses to kiss her arse. She hops off her horse and pulls down her pants. While the knight should have recognized her female genitalia, he remarks that she has a long arse. Before she leaves, she tells him, "I'm Bérangier of the Long Ass, Who puts shame to the chickenhearted." The wife returns home and sleeps with a valiant knight. When her husband arrives from the forest, he rebukes her. However, that was his last demeaning remark to her. She tells him she met Bérangier and learned of her husband's cowardice. To protect his own name, the knight is forced to succumb to his wife's wishes. Her cleverness leads her to do as she pleased for the rest of her life, and her husband lives in shame.[^35]
