A **narrative technique** or **narrative device** (also, in [fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiction "Fiction"), a **fictional device**) is any of several [storytelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Storytelling "Storytelling") [techniques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_device "Literary device") that the creator of a [story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrative "Narrative") uses,[^1] thus effectively relaying information to the audience or making the story more complete, complex, or engaging. Some scholars also call such a technique a **narrative mode**, though this term can also more narrowly refer to the particular technique of [using a commentary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narration "Narration") to deliver a story. Narrative techniques are distinguished from [narrative elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrative#Basic_elements "Narrative"), which exist inherently in all works of narrative, rather than being merely optional strategies.

## Setting

| Name | Definition | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Setting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Setting_\(narrative\) "Setting (narrative)") as a form of [symbolism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artistic_symbol "Artistic symbol") or [allegory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegory "Allegory") | The setting is both the time and geographic location within a narrative or within a work of fiction; sometimes, storytellers use the setting as a way to represent deeper ideas, reflect characters' emotions, or encourage the audience to make certain connections that add complexity to how the story may be interpreted. | The novel *[Ulysses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_\(novel\) "Ulysses (novel)")* by James Joyce is set in [Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin "Dublin"), [Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland "Ireland"), over the course of a single day, 16 June 1904. The novel spans from [Sandycove](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandycove "Sandycove") in the South to [Howth Head](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howth_Head "Howth Head") in the North, with detailed descriptions of [Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin "Dublin") locations that parallel those in [Homer's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer "Homer") *[Odyssey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odyssey "Odyssey")*. [Leopold Bloom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leopold_Bloom "Leopold Bloom") 's home at [7 Eccles Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7_Eccles_Street "7 Eccles Street") serves as a modern-day [Ithaca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homer%27s_Ithaca "Homer's Ithaca"), while other landmarks represent various episodes of the Odyssey. In [Tayeb Salih](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tayeb_Salih "Tayeb Salih") 's *[Season of Migration to the North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Season_of_Migration_to_the_North "Season of Migration to the North")* (1966), the narrative alternates between an unnamed village on the [Nile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nile "Nile") and [London, England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London "London"). This dual setting frames Mustafa Sa'eed's movement between home and the former imperial center. It shows how place shapes his relationships and sense of self while the novel examines the continuing effects of colonialism.[^2] |

## Plot

| Name | Definition | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Backstory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backstory "Backstory") | Story that precedes events in the story being told—past events or background that add meaning to current circumstances | Though *[The Lord of the Rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lord_of_the_Rings "The Lord of the Rings")* trilogy takes place in a relatively short period towards the end of the 3021-year Third Age, the narration gives glimpses of the mythological and historical events which took place earlier in the Third Age leading up to the action in the novel, and in the First and Second Age. |
| [Chekhov's gun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chekhov%27s_gun "Chekhov's gun") | A dramatic principle that requires every element in a narrative to be substantive, with anything redundant or arbitrary removed. | "Remove everything that has no relevance to the story. If you say in the first chapter that there is a rifle hanging on the wall, in the second or third chapter it absolutely must go off. If it's not going to be fired, it shouldn't be hanging there." —  Anton Chekhov |
| [Cliffhanger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cliffhanger "Cliffhanger") | The narrative ends unresolved, to draw the audience back to a future episode for the resolution. | Almost every episode of TV shows like *[Dexter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dexter_\(TV_series\) "Dexter (TV series)")* and *[Breaking Bad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breaking_Bad "Breaking Bad")* [^3] ends with one of the characters in a predicament (about to be caught by thugs, about to be exposed by the authorities, or a family member or a friend finds out the main character's dirty secret). |
| [Eucatastrophe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eucatastrophe "Eucatastrophe") | Coined by [J. R. R. Tolkien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._R._R._Tolkien "J. R. R. Tolkien"), a climactic event through which the protagonist appears to be facing a catastrophic change. However, this change does not materialize and the protagonist finds themself benefiting from the climactic event; contrast [peripety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peripety "Peripety") / *peripateia*. | At the end of *The Lord of the Rings*, Gollum forcibly takes away the Ring from Frodo, suggesting that [Sauron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sauron "Sauron") would eventually take over Middle Earth. However, Gollum celebrates too eagerly and clumsily falls into the lava, whereby the ring is destroyed and with it Sauron's power. In a way, Gollum does what Frodo and the Fellowship of the Ring intended to do through the whole plot of the trilogy, which was to throw the ring into the lake of fire in the heart of Mount Doom. |
| [Flashback](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flashback_\(narrative\) "Flashback (narrative)") (or analepsis) | Alteration of time sequences, taking characters back to the beginning of the tale, for instance | The story of " [The Three Apples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Three_Apples "The Three Apples") " in the *[Arabian Nights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_Nights "Arabian Nights")* tales begins with the discovery of a young woman's dead body. After the murderer later reveals himself, he narrates his reasons for the murder as a flashback of events leading up to the discovery of her dead body at the beginning of the story. |
| [Flashforward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flashforward "Flashforward") (or prolepsis) | A scene that temporarily jumps the narrative forward in time. A flashforward often represents events expected, projected, or imagined to occur in the future. They may also reveal significant parts of the story that have not yet occurred, but soon will in greater detail. | Occurs in *[A Christmas Carol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Christmas_Carol "A Christmas Carol")* when Mr. Scrooge visits the ghost of the future. It is also frequent in the later seasons of the television series [*Lost*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_\(2004_TV_series\) "Lost (2004 TV series)"). |
| [Foreshadowing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreshadowing "Foreshadowing") | Implicit yet intentional efforts of an author to suggest events that have yet to take place in the process of narration. See also [repetitive designation](https://en.wikipedia.org/w/index.php?title=Repetitive_designation&action=edit&redlink=1 "Repetitive designation (page does not exist)") and [Chekhov's gun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chekhov%27s_gun "Chekhov's gun") | A narration might begin with a male character who has to break up a schoolyard fight among some boys who are vying for the attention of a girl, which was introduced to foreshadow the events leading to a dinner time squabble between the character and his twin brother over a woman, whom both are courting at the same time. |
| [Frame story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_story "Frame story") | A story which occupies a small part of the total narrative, such that one or more other stories are recounted within the frame story. Contrast [story within a story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Story_within_a_story "Story within a story"). | With multiple internal stories: early examples include *[Panchatantra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panchatantra "Panchatantra")*, *[Kalila and Dimna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalila_and_Dimna "Kalila and Dimna")*, *[Arabian Nights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Book_of_One_Thousand_and_One_Nights "The Book of One Thousand and One Nights")*, and *[The Decameron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Decameron "The Decameron")*. More modern examples are [Brian Jacques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Jacques "Brian Jacques") 's 1999 *[The Legend of Luke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Legend_of_Luke "The Legend of Luke")*, [Ramsay Wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramsay_Wood "Ramsay Wood") 's 2011 *[Kalila and Dimna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalila_and_Dimna "Kalila and Dimna")* update, subtitled *Fables of Conflict and Intrigue* and [Sophia de Mello Breyner Andresen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophia_de_Mello_Breyner_Andresen "Sophia de Mello Breyner Andresen") 's 1964 *The Knight of Denmark* (O cavaleiro da Dinamarca).  With a single internal story: in *[Frankenstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankenstein "Frankenstein")*, the frame story is narrated by an Arctic explorer, who is told the main story by Victor Frankenstein. In *[Wuthering Heights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wuthering_Heights "Wuthering Heights")*, the frame story is told by a visitor; the main story begins 30 years earlier and comprises transcripts of letters and journal entries he reads and accounts he hears from the protagonists. |
| *[In medias res](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/In_medias_res "In medias res")* | Beginning the story in the middle of a sequence of events. A specific form of narrative hook. | This is used in epic poems, for example, where it is a mandatory form to be adopted. Luís de Camões' *The Lusiads* or the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey* of Homer are prime examples. The latter work begins with the return of Odysseus to his home of Ithaca and then in flashbacks tells of his ten years of wandering following the Trojan War. *The Lusiads* starts in the middle of the sea voyage to India and contextualizes the beginning of said journey as well as Portugal's history when the master of the ship tells an African king about it. |
| [Narrative hook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrative_hook "Narrative hook") | Story opening that "hooks" readers' attention so they will keep reading | Many non-fiction books are introduced with an interesting factoid. |
| [MacGuffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacGuffin "MacGuffin") | Object required to initiate the plot or motivation of the characters, but having little significance by itself | "Heart of the Ocean" necklace in James Cameron's 1997 Titanic, which essentially serves as an object to cause Rose to tell her story. |
| [Ochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ochi "Ochi") | A sudden interruption of the wordplay flow indicating the end of a [rakugo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rakugo "Rakugo") or a kobanashi. | A Rakugo is a Japanese verbal entertainment usually lasting 30 minutes which ends with a surprise punch line, a narrative stunt known as ochi (fall) or sage (lowering). Twelve kinds of ochi are codified and recognized. The earlier *kobanashi* was a short comical vignette ending with an ochi. |
| [Plot twist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plot_twist "Plot twist") | Unexpected change ("twist") in the direction or expected outcome of the plot. See also [twist ending](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twist_ending "Twist ending"). | An early example is the *[Arabian Nights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_Nights "Arabian Nights")* tale " [The Three Apples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Three_Apples "The Three Apples") ". A locked chest found by a fisherman contains a dead body, and two different men claim to be the murderer, which turns out to be the investigator's own slave. |
| [Poetic justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetic_justice "Poetic justice") | Virtue ultimately rewarded, or vice punished, by an ironic twist of fate related to the character's own conduct | [Wile E. Coyote](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wile_E._Coyote_and_the_Road_Runner "Wile E. Coyote and the Road Runner") coming up with a contraption to catch the Road Runner, only to be foiled and caught by his own devices. Each sin's punishment in [Dante's *Inferno*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inferno_\(Dante\) "Inferno (Dante)") is a symbolic instance of poetic justice. |
| [Predestination paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predestination_paradox "Predestination paradox") | Time travel paradox where a time traveler is caught in a loop of events that "predestines" them to travel back in time | In *[Doctor Who](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Who "Doctor Who")*, the main character repeatedly finds himself under the obligation of having to travel back in time because of something his future character has done. |
| [Red herring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Red_herring "Red herring") | Diverting attention away from an item of significance. | For example, in mystery fiction, an innocent party may be purposefully cast as highly suspicious through emphasis or descriptive techniques to divert attention from the true guilty party. |
| [Self-fulfilling prophecy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-fulfilling_prophecy "Self-fulfilling prophecy") | Prediction that, by being made, makes itself come true. | Early examples include the legend of [Oedipus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oedipus "Oedipus"), and the story of [Krishna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krishna "Krishna") in the *[Mahabharata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahabharata "Mahabharata")*. There is also an example of this in *[Harry Potter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Potter "Harry Potter")* when Lord Voldemort heard a prophecy (made by Sybill Trelawney to Dumbledore) that a boy born at the end of July, whose parents had defied Voldemort thrice and survived, would be made marked as his equal. Because of this prophecy, Lord Voldemort sought out Harry Potter (believing him to be the boy spoken of) and tried to kill him. His parents died protecting him, and when Voldemort tried to cast a killing curse on Harry, it rebounded and took away most of his strength, and gave Harry Potter a unique ability and connection with the Dark Lord thus marking him as his equal. |
| *[Story within a story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Story_within_a_story "Story within a story")* (Hypodiegesis) | A story told within another story. See also [frame story](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_story "Frame story"). | In Stephen King's *The Wind Through the Keyhole*, of the *Dark Tower* series, the protagonist tells a story from his past to his companions, and in this story he tells another relatively unrelated story. |
| [Ticking time bomb scenario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ticking_time_bomb_scenario "Ticking time bomb scenario") | Threat of impending disaster—often used in thrillers where salvation and escape are essential elements | In the [post-apocalyptic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_holocaust "Nuclear holocaust") novel *[On the Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Beach_\(novel\) "On the Beach (novel)")*, the main characters face increasing [radioactivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fallout "Nuclear fallout") drifting [across the equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intertropical_Convergence_Zone "Intertropical Convergence Zone") toward Australia. Learning that the [worst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_radiation_syndrome "Acute radiation syndrome") is predicted to come sooner rather than later heightens the urgency and sense of immediacy felt by the characters and by the reader. |
| [Unreliable narrator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unreliable_narrator "Unreliable narrator") | The narrator of the story is not sincere, or introduces a bias in their narration and possibly misleads the reader, hiding or minimizing events, characters, or motivations. | An example is *[The Murder of Roger Ackroyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Murder_of_Roger_Ackroyd "The Murder of Roger Ackroyd")*. The novel includes an unexpected [plot twist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plot_twist "Plot twist") at the end of the novel. In the last chapter, Sheppard describes how he was an [unreliable narrator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unreliable_narrator "Unreliable narrator"). |

## Perspective

| Name | Definition | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Audience surrogate | A character who expresses the questions and confusion of the audience, with whom the audience can identify. Frequently used in detective fiction and science fiction, where the character asks a central character how they accomplished certain deeds, for the purpose of inciting that character to explain (for the curious audience) his or her methods, or a character asking a relatively educated person to explain what amounts to the backstory. | [Dr. Watson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dr._Watson "Dr. Watson") in the Sherlock Holmes stories. Scott Evil, played by [Seth Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seth_Green "Seth Green"), son of Dr. Evil in the *[Austin Powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austin_Powers "Austin Powers")* films. The [companion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Companion_\(Doctor_Who\) "Companion (Doctor Who)") role in *[Doctor Who](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_Who "Doctor Who")*, usually a contemporary human, giving the alien Doctor someone to explain situations to, for the benefit of the audience. [Dr. Jennifer Melfi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dr._Jennifer_Melfi "Dr. Jennifer Melfi") in *[The Sopranos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sopranos "The Sopranos")*. |
| [Author surrogate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Author_surrogate "Author surrogate") | Characters which are based on authors, usually to support their personal views. Sometimes an intentionally or unintentionally idealized version of them. A variation is the [Mary Sue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Sue "Mary Sue") or Gary Stu, which primarily serves as an idealized self-insertion. | Socrates in the writings of Plato. Plato never speaks in his own voice in his dialogues. In the [*Second Letter*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Letter_\(Plato\) "Second Letter (Plato)"), it says, "no writing of Plato exists or ever will exist, but those now said to be his are those of a Socrates become beautiful and new". |
| Breaking the [fourth wall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourth_wall "Fourth wall") | An author or character addresses the audience directly (also known as direct address). This may acknowledge to the reader or audience that what is being presented is fiction, or may seek to extend the world of the story to provide the illusion that they are included in it. | The characters in *[Sesame Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sesame_Street "Sesame Street")* often break the fourth wall when they address their viewers as part of the ongoing storyline, which is possible because of the high level of [suspension of disbelief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspension_of_disbelief "Suspension of disbelief") afforded by its audience—children. The English political drama show *[House of Cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Cards_\(U.S._TV_series\) "House of Cards (U.S. TV series)")* and its later American version, also use this technique frequently to let the viewers know what the main character [Frank Underwood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Underwood_\(House_of_Cards\) "Frank Underwood (House of Cards)") is thinking and planning. Ferris Bueller in *[Ferris Bueller's Day Off](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferris_Bueller%27s_Day_Off "Ferris Bueller's Day Off")* frequently addresses the audience. Mr. Wolf in *[The Bad Guys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bad_Guys_\(film\) "The Bad Guys (film)")* and its [sequel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Bad_Guys_2 "The Bad Guys 2") occasionally talks to the audience. |
| [Defamiliarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defamiliarization "Defamiliarization") | Taking an everyday object and presenting it in a way that is weirdly unfamiliar so that we see the object in a new way. Coined by the early 20th-century Russian literary critic Viktor Shklovsky in "Art as Technique." | In Swift's *[Gulliver's Travels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulliver%27s_Travels "Gulliver's Travels")*, when Gulliver visits the land of the giants and sees a giant woman's skin, he sees it as anything but smooth and beautiful when viewed up close.[^4] Another common method of defamiliarization is to "make strange" a story ([fabula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabula_and_syuzhet "Fabula and syuzhet")) by creating a deformed plot (syuzhet). Tristram Shandy is defamiliarized by [Laurence Sterne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurence_Sterne "Laurence Sterne") 's unfamiliar plotting,[^5] which causes the reader to pay attention to the story and see it in an unjaded way. |
| [First-person narration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-person_narrative "First-person narrative") | A text presented from the point of view of a character, especially the protagonist, as if the character is telling the story themselves. (Breaking the fourth wall is an option, but not a necessity, of this format.) | [Mark Twain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Twain "Mark Twain") 's *[Adventures of Huckleberry Finn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adventures_of_Huckleberry_Finn "Adventures of Huckleberry Finn")* uses the title character as the narrator, while *[Sherlock Holmes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherlock_Holmes "Sherlock Holmes")* is primarily told from Watson's perspective. The film *[The Wolf of Wall Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Wolf_of_Wall_Street_\(2013_film\) "The Wolf of Wall Street (2013 film)")* uses this technique where the protagonist narrates the film's events throughout, providing clarity that could not be gained from the picture and dialogue alone. |
| [Magical realism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_realism "Magic realism") | Describing events in a real-world setting but with magical trappings, often incorporating local customs and invented beliefs. Different from [urban fantasy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_fantasy "Urban fantasy") in that the magic itself is not the focus of the story. | Particularly popular with [Latin American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_America "Latin America") authors like [Gabriel García Márquez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Garc%C3%ADa_M%C3%A1rquez "Gabriel García Márquez") and [Jorge Luis Borges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jorge_Luis_Borges "Jorge Luis Borges"). Elsewhere, [Salman Rushdie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salman_Rushdie "Salman Rushdie") 's work provides good examples. |
| [Multiperspectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiperspectivity "Multiperspectivity") | A narrative that is told from the [viewpoints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrative_point_of_view "Narrative point of view") of multiple characters that incorporate various perspectives, emotions, and views from witnesses or actors to varying particular events or circumstances that might not be felt by other characters in the story. | The films of [Robert Altman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Altman "Robert Altman"). *2666* by [Roberto Bolano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roberto_Bola%C3%B1o "Roberto Bolaño") features European literary critics, a Chilean philosophy professor, an African-American journalist, detectives investigating Santa Teresa murders and an obscure German writer named Benno Von Archimboldi. *[Pale Fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pale_Fire "Pale Fire")* by Vladimir Nabokov features literature professor John Shade, Charles Kinbote, a neighbor and colleague of Shade's and Charles the Beloved, king of Zembla. Kinbote is the ultimate unreliable commentator. |
| [Second-person narration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second-person_narration "Second-person narration") | A text written in the style of a direct address, in the second-person. | *[Bright Lights, Big City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bright_Lights,_Big_City_\(novel\) "Bright Lights, Big City (novel)")* by [Jay McInerney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jay_McInerney "Jay McInerney"). |
| [Stream of consciousness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stream_of_consciousness "Stream of consciousness") | The author uses narrative and stylistic devices to create the sense of an unedited [interior monologue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interior_monologue "Interior monologue"), characterized by leaps in syntax and punctuation that trace a character's fragmentary thoughts and sensory feelings. The outcome is a highly lucid perspective with a plot. Not to be confused with [free writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_writing "Free writing"). | An example is *[Ulysses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysses_\(novel\) "Ulysses (novel)")*. At one point Leopold Bloom saunters through Dublin musing on "Pineapple rock, lemon platt, butter scotch. A sugar-sticky girl shovelling scoopful of creams for a Christian brother. Some school treat. Bad for their tummies." |
| [Third-person narration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third-person_narrative "Third-person narrative") | A text written as if by an impersonal narrator who is not affected by the events in the story. Can be omniscient or limited, the latter usually being tied to a specific character, a group of characters, or a location. | *[A Song of Ice and Fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Song_of_Ice_and_Fire "A Song of Ice and Fire")* is written in multiple limited third-person narrators that change with each chapter. *[The Master and Margarita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Master_and_Margarita "The Master and Margarita")* uses an omniscient narrator. |

## Style

| Name | Definition | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Allegory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allegory "Allegory") | The expression, by means of symbolic fictional figures and actions, of truths or generalizations about human conduct or experience [^6] | C. S. Lewis's [*The Lion, the Witch, and the Wardrobe*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Lion,_the_Witch_and_the_Wardrobe "The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe") is a religious **allegory** with Aslan as Christ and Edmund as Judas.[^7] |
| [Alliteration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alliteration "Alliteration") | Repeating the same letter or consonant sound at the beginning of adjacent or closely connected words. | In the film *[V for Vendetta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V_for_Vendetta_\(film\) "V for Vendetta (film)")* the main character performs a couple of soliloquies with a heavy use of alliteration, e.g., "Voilà! In view, a humble vaudevillian veteran, cast vicariously as both victim and villain by the vicissitudes of Fate. This visage, no mere veneer of vanity, is a vestige of the vox populi, now vacant, vanished, as the once vital voice of the verisimilitude now venerates what they once vilified. However, this valorous visitation of a bygone vexation stands vivified, and has vowed to vanquish these venal and virulent vermin vanguarding vice and vouchsafing the violently vicious and voracious violation of volition. The only verdict is vengeance; a vendetta held as a votive, not in vain, for the value and veracity of such shall one day vindicate the vigilant and the virtuous. Verily, this vichyssoise of verbiage veers most verbose vis-à-vis an introduction, and so it is my very good honor to meet you and you may call me V." |
| [Amplification (rhetoric)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplification_\(rhetoric\) "Amplification (rhetoric)") | Amplification refers to a literary practice wherein the writer embellishes the sentence by adding more information to it in order to increase its worth and understanding. | E.g., Original sentence: The thesis paper was difficult. After amplification: The thesis paper was difficult: it required extensive research, data collection, sample surveys, interviews and a lot of fieldwork. |
| [Anagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anagram "Anagram") | Rearranging the letters of a word or a phrase to form a new phrase or word. | E.g., An anagram for "debit card" is "bad credit". As you can see, both phrases use the same letters. By mixing the letters a bit of humor is created. |
| [Asyndeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asyndeton "Asyndeton") | When sentences do not use conjunctions (e.g., and, or, nor) to separate clauses, but run clauses into one another, usually marking the separation of clauses with punctuation. | An example is when [John F. Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy "John F. Kennedy") said on January 20, 1961 "...that we shall pay any price, bear any burden, meet any hardship, support any friend, oppose any foe to assure the survival and the success of liberty." |
| [Bathos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bathos "Bathos") | An abrupt transition in style from the exalted to the commonplace, producing a ludicrous effect. While often unintended, bathos may be used deliberately to produce a humorous effect.[^8] [^9] | The ballerina rose gracefully en pointe and extended one slender leg behind her, like a dog at a fire hydrant.[^10] [^11] |
| [Caesura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesura "Caesura") | A break, especially a sense pause, usually near the middle of a verse, and marked in [scansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scansion "Scansion") by a double vertical line. This technique frequently occurs within a poetic line grammatically connected to the end of the previous line by [enjambment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enjambment "Enjambment"). | E.g., in "Know then thyself. ‖ Presume not God to scan" (from *[An Essay on Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Essay_on_Man "An Essay on Man")* by [Alexander Pope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Pope "Alexander Pope")) |
| [Distancing effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distancing_effect "Distancing effect") | Deliberately preventing the audience from identifying with characters in order to let them be coolly scrutinized.[^12] | Popularized by 20th century playwright [Bertolt Brecht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertolt_Brecht "Bertolt Brecht"). |
| Dramatic visualization | Representing an object or character with abundant descriptive detail, or mimetically rendering gestures and dialogue to make a scene more visual or imaginatively present to an audience. | This technique appears at least as far back as the *[Arabian Nights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Thousand_and_One_Nights "One Thousand and One Nights")*.[^13] |
| [Euphuism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphuism "Euphuism") | An artificial, highly elaborate way of writing or speaking. Named from *Euphues* (1579) the prose romance by [John Lyly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lyly "John Lyly"). | "Is it not far better to abhor sins by the remembrance of others' faults, than by repentance of thine own follies?" (from [Euphues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euphues "Euphues"), 1, lecture by the wise Neapolitan) |
| [Hyperbole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbole "Hyperbole") | Exaggeration used to evoke strong feelings or create an impression which is not meant to be taken literally. Hyperbole can be used for serious, ironic, or comic effects.[^14] | Sally could no longer hide her secret. Her pregnant belly was bigger than the planet on which she stood. |
| [Imagery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imagery "Imagery") | Forming mental images of a scene using descriptive words, especially making use of the human senses. The same as *sensory detail.* | When the boots came off his feet with a leathery squeak, a smell of ferment and fish market immediately filled the small tent. The skin of his toes were red and raw and sensitive. The malodorous air was so toxic he thought he could almost taste his toes. |
| [Leitwortstil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leitwortstil "Leitwortstil") | Purposefully repeating words that usually express a motif or [theme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theme_\(literature\) "Theme (literature)") important to the story. | This dates back at least to the *[Arabian Nights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Thousand_and_One_Nights "One Thousand and One Nights")*.[^15] |
| [Metonymy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metonymy "Metonymy") | Word or phrase in a figure of speech in which a noun is referenced by something closely associated with it, rather than explicitly by the noun itself. This is not to be confused with [synecdoche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synecdoche "Synecdoche"), in which a part of the whole stands for the thing itself. | Metonymy: The boxer threw in the towel. Synecdoche: She gave her hand in marriage. |
| [Overstatement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbole "Hyperbole") | Exaggerating something, often for emphasis (also known as [hyperbole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbole "Hyperbole")) | Sally's pregnant belly most likely weighed as much as the scooter she used to ride before she got pregnant. |
| [Onomatopoeia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onomatopoeia "Onomatopoeia") | Words that imitate/spell a sound or noise.  Word that sounds the same as, or similar to what the word means. | "Boom goes the dynamite." "Bang!" "Bark." (comic books) |
| [Oxymoron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxymoron "Oxymoron") | A term made of two words that deliberately or coincidentally imply each other's opposite. | "terrible beauty" |
| [Paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradox_\(literature\) "Paradox (literature)") | A phrase that describes an idea composed of concepts that conflict. | "It was the best of times, it was the worst of times." (*A Tale of Two Cities*) |
| [Parody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parody "Parody") | Ridicule by overstated imitation, usually humorous. | *[MAD Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MAD_Magazine "MAD Magazine")* |
| [Pastiche](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pastiche "Pastiche") | Using forms and styles from another author, generally as an affectionate tribute. | Such as the many stories featuring [Sherlock Holmes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sherlock_Holmes "Sherlock Holmes") not written by [Arthur Conan Doyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Conan_Doyle "Arthur Conan Doyle"), or much of the [Cthulhu Mythos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_Mythos "Cthulhu Mythos"). |
| [Pathos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathos "Pathos") | Emotional appeal, one of the three [modes of persuasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modes_of_persuasion "Modes of persuasion") in rhetoric that the author uses to inspire pity or sorrow towards a character—typically does not counterbalance the target character's suffering with a positive outcome, as in Tragedy. | In *Romeo and Juliet*, the two main characters each commit suicide at the sight of the supposedly dead lover, however the audience knows these actions to be rash and unnecessary. Therefore, Shakespeare makes for the emotional appeal for the unnecessary tragedy behind the young characters' rash interpretations about love and life. |
| [Personification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personification "Personification") | Using comparative metaphors and similes to give characteristics to abstract concepts. | Taken from Act I, Scene II of *[Romeo and Juliet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romeo_and_Juliet "Romeo and Juliet")*: "When well-appareled April on the heel / Of limping winter treads." [^16] |
| [Polyptoton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyptoton "Polyptoton") | Words derived from the same root in a sentence. | "Not as a call to **battle**, though **embattled** we are." [John F. Kennedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_F._Kennedy "John F. Kennedy"), *Inaugural Address*, January 20, 1961. |
| [Polysyndeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polysyndeton "Polysyndeton") | Polysyndeton is the use of several conjunctions in close succession. This provides a sense of exaggeration designed to wear down the audience. | An example of this is in the first chapter of *[Great Expectations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Expectations "Great Expectations")* by [Charles Dickens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Dickens "Charles Dickens"): "A man who had been soaked in water, and smothered in mud, and lamed by stones, and cut by flints, and stung by nettles, and torn by briars; who limped, and shivered, and glared and growled; and whose teeth chattered in his head as he seized me by the chin." |
| [Satire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satire "Satire") | The use of humor, irony or exaggeration to criticize. | An example is *[Network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_\(1976_film\) "Network (1976 film)")*. One of the earliest examples is *Gulliver's Travels*, written by [Jonathan Swift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Swift "Jonathan Swift"). The television program *[South Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Park "South Park")* is another. |
| [Sensory detail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_imagery "Mental imagery") | Sight, sound, taste, touch, smell. The same as *imagery*. | The boot was tough and sinewy between his hard-biting teeth. There was no flavor to speak of except for the blandness of all the dirt that the boot had soaked up over the years. The only thing the boot reminded him of was the smell of a wet dog. |
| [Understatement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Understatement "Understatement") | A diminishing or softening of a theme for effect. | The broken ends of the long bone were sticking through the bleeding skin, but it wasn't something that always killed a man. |
| [Title drop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Title_drop "Title drop") | Line of dialogue used to announce the name of the piece. | In *[The Breakfast Club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Breakfast_Club "The Breakfast Club")*, the final line is "Sincerely yours, The Breakfast Club". |

## Theme

| Name | Definition | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [Irony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irony "Irony") | This discrepancy between expectation and reality occurs in three forms: situational irony, where a situation features a discrepancy between what is expected and what is actualized; dramatic irony, where a character is unaware of pivotal information already revealed to the audience (the discrepancy here lies in the two levels of awareness between the character and the audience); and verbal irony, where one states one thing while meaning another. The difference between verbal irony and sarcasm is exquisitely subtle and often contested. The concept of irony is too often misunderstood in popular usage. Unfortunate circumstances and coincidences do not constitute irony (nor do they qualify as being *tragic*). See the *Usage controversy* section under [irony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irony "Irony"), and the term [tragedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tragedy "Tragedy"). | [A person hears a prophecy about himself. His endeavor to stop the prophecy from coming true makes it come true.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-fulfilling_prophecy "Self-fulfilling prophecy") |
| [Metaphor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphor "Metaphor") | Evoking imagination by means of using figurative language. | Her tears were a river flowing down her cheeks. |
| [Thematic patterning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thematic_patterning "Thematic patterning") | Distributing recurrent thematic concepts and moralistic [motifs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motif_\(narrative\) "Motif (narrative)") among various incidents and frames of a story. In a skillfully crafted tale, thematic patterning may emphasize the unifying argument or salient idea that disparate events and frames have in common. | Each of the chapters of *Ulysses* by James Joyce. |
