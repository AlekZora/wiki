## 2\. What is actually going wrong for you?

From your description, the sequence seems to be something like:

**Goal appears → possibilities proliferate → you mentally simulate them → notice weaknesses → reject them → generate alternatives → compare alternatives → none feels sufficiently justified → no external action occurs.**

The important part is this:

You are **doing a lot of cognitive work**.

It just happens entirely before contact with reality.

That is why it can feel strange. You are not lazy, unmotivated, or incapable of sustained effort. Once reality gives you a defined task, your execution system works extremely well.

Your bottleneck is probably the transition:

> **possibility → commitment**

The mind keeps treating the decision as something that must be solved intellectually before action begins.

But many self-directed projects cannot be solved that way because the information required to make the correct decision **only appears after you begin**.

That creates a trap:

> “I need enough information to choose correctly.”

while reality works like:

> “You need to choose provisionally in order to obtain the information.”

That distinction is crucial.

## What I would change

You probably don't need generic advice like "be more disciplined."

Instead, you need to redesign the **decision architecture before execution begins**.

### A. Stop asking, “What should I build?”

Replace it with:

> **“What is the smallest experiment I can run that would teach me something?”**

This is much easier psychologically because you're no longer committing to an identity, company, product, or long-term path.

Suppose you have three product ideas.

Your current brain might ask:

> Which one is most original?  
> Which has the best market?  
> Which can become a company?  
> Which fits AI best?  
> Which is defensible?

You could spend six weeks answering those.

Instead:

> Which one can I put in front of 5 strangers within 7 days?

Now you're dealing with an executable problem.

---

### B. Separate selection mode from execution mode

This may be particularly important for you.

Give yourself a fixed selection window.

For example:

**Monday, 10:00–11:00:** choose what to work on.

At 11:00, selection ends.

Whatever survives becomes the project until Friday.

During execution you are **not allowed to reopen the question of whether another idea is better**.

You can write competing ideas down, but you don't switch.

This matters because otherwise the brain runs two processes simultaneously:

> building the thing  
> evaluating whether the thing deserves to exist

That is cognitively poisonous.

---

### C. Make decisions explicitly temporary

Instead of:

> “I've decided to build X.”

say:

> **“For the next 14 days, X is the hypothesis.”**

This is a surprisingly powerful change.

Your brain appears to assign too much significance to initial decisions. If choosing A implicitly means rejecting B, C, D, and E forever, the decision becomes enormous.

But:

> A until September 18

is much easier than:

> A is my future.

Fred and George don't need to know that every product will work. They make one, test it, discard bad ones, continue.

---

### D. Force reality into the loop much earlier

This may be the single biggest intervention for you.

You've described a tendency to start and reject projects **inside your head**.

You need a rule:

> **An idea is not allowed to be rejected before producing an external artifact.**

An external artifact can be tiny:

- one screen,
- one working function,
- a 200-word concept,
- one generated quest,
- a landing page,
- a five-minute prototype,
- one conversation with a potential user.

Only after that may you say:

> This is bad.

Because before that, you're evaluating an imaginary object.

Imaginary projects are extremely easy to kill because your mind can instantly imagine a better one.

Real things are different. They produce evidence.

---

### E. Define success as learning, not choosing correctly

A failed two-day experiment isn't failure.

If it tells you:

> People don't care about this mechanic,

you have acquired information.

But spending three weeks trying to determine whether people *would* care produces almost nothing.

So your internal metric needs to move from:

> **Did I choose the right thing?**

to:

> **Did reality teach me something this week?**

That is much closer to how founders, scientists, designers, and inventors actually operate.

## And there's a deeper psychological distinction

Harry's world continuously provides him with **stakes**.

Someone is in danger.  
Something strange happened.  
A person disappeared.  
Voldemort is coming.

The environment removes ambiguity.

In self-directed work there is no Voldemort.

Nothing happens if you do nothing today.

So you have to manufacture something Harry receives automatically:

**a concrete situation requiring a response.**

This may be why externally assigned tasks work so well for you. Someone else has already done three cognitively expensive things:

**defined the objective → removed alternatives → established legitimacy**

Then your execution ability activates.

So the long-term goal isn't simply "be more motivated."

It is to become good at doing those three things **for yourself**.

And this is why I think Fred and George are actually a better model for you than Harry.

Their defining characteristic isn't courage.

It's **experimental agency**:

> “This might work. Let's make one and see.”

For someone with your particular pattern, that sentence may be considerably more useful than "just take action."

You should probably stop treating motivation as something that must stay high continuously.

Your pattern sounds more like **episodic activation**: fiction gives you a strong emotional charge, a sense of possibility, identity, adventure, competence, or destiny—and then that state fades. That is normal. The mistake is expecting the emotional peak to carry the project.

What you need is to use the peak to create **structure that survives the peak**.

For you, fiction can actually be useful if you use it deliberately. Harry Potter, Mass Effect, Star Wars, etc. seem to activate a mode in you where the world feels larger, action feels meaningful, and you identify with people who move toward difficult goals. So use that as an ignition mechanism rather than dismissing it as escapism.

A practical model:

**1\. Let fiction trigger the session.**  
Watch a scene, listen to a soundtrack, read a few pages—something that reliably creates that sense of momentum.

**2\. Immediately convert the feeling into one physical action.**  
Not “plan the company.” Something like:

- open the project,
- write one mechanic,
- build one interaction,
- make one screen,
- test one hypothesis.

The transition needs to be fast. Otherwise the emotional energy gets consumed by thinking.

**3\. Stop while you still have some motivation left.**  
This sounds counterintuitive, but it helps. If you always work until you are exhausted, your brain starts associating the project with depletion. Ending after a concrete win makes the next session easier.

**4\. Leave yourself an obvious re-entry point.**  
At the end, write one sentence:

> “Next: make the memory tile merge when two identical states collide.”

Then tomorrow you do not need to decide what to do. You only resume.

That last part is especially important for you because **decision-making itself drains you**.

I would also separate two concepts:

**Motivation:** “I strongly feel like doing this.”  
**Momentum:** “I already know what the next action is, so continuing is easy.”

You probably cannot reliably control the first. You can design the second.

And given what you’ve told me, I would intentionally make your projects feel more like **quests** than vague obligations. Not in a childish way—structurally.

Instead of:

> “Work on startup.”

use:

> “Mission: by tonight, make the AI remember one thing across two interactions.”

Instead of:

> “Figure out game idea.”

use:

> “Mission: make one mechanic that is fun for 60 seconds.”

That works because fiction tends to give goals **stakes, boundaries, and a clear next move**. Your self-directed work often lacks exactly those properties.

One warning: do not let fiction become another form of preparation. If you spend three hours consuming inspiring material and then do nothing, it has become stimulation rather than ignition.

So I would use a simple rule:

> **Inspiration must cash out into action within 15 minutes.**

Even five minutes of building counts.

The deeper point is that you may not need “more motivation.” You may need to become better at **capturing motivational spikes before they evaporate**.

That is a much more manageable problem.

Yes. I think there is a coherent roadmap for this, and I would define the target more precisely than “become entrepreneurial.”

You want to develop **self-directed agency**:

> **Notice → choose → commit → act → observe → adapt → finish → choose again.**

You already appear relatively strong in the **act / solve / adapt** portion once a task has been defined. The training should therefore disproportionately target the other parts.

I would work through it like this:

1. **Train self-assignment first.** For 2 weeks, give yourself one small mission every day that nobody asked you to do. It should take 20–60 minutes and have a concrete finish line: “Make one playable interaction,” “write one landing page,” “contact one potential user.” The subject barely matters. You are training the neurological/behavioral sequence **I decided → therefore I acted**.
2. **Train choosing without proof.** Give decisions explicit deadlines. For small decisions: 15–30 minutes. For prototype directions: perhaps 1–2 days. When the deadline arrives, choose something that is *plausible*, not demonstrably optimal. You specifically need repetitions of making decisions while still thinking, “I'm not sure.”
3. **Turn prototyping into part of reasoning.** This is the principle you already liked. When you encounter a question such as “Would this mechanic actually work?”, don't automatically produce another branch of reasoning. Ask: **“Can I answer this by making something?”** Code, mockups, sketches, conversations and experiments become extensions of thought.
4. **Learn to commit temporarily.** Your decisions don't need to be permanent. Say: **“For the next three days, this decision is closed.”** New ideas go into a parking-lot document. You don't switch. After three days, reopen the decision using the evidence you've acquired. This is especially important for someone whose mind can generate a better alternative every five minutes.
5. **Shrink the planning horizon.** Don't mentally execute the entire company. Define the destination broadly, but plan only the next observable milestone. “Build an AI game company” is useless operationally. “By Sunday, another person can play the memory-merging mechanic for five minutes” is excellent. After reaching that state, determine the next one.
6. **Build contact with reality into every project.** Your mind is capable of generating hundreds of hypothetical outcomes. Reality can eliminate most of them very cheaply. Therefore, every week something must leave your head/computer: another person plays it, sees it, uses it, rejects it, pays for it, comments on it, etc. I'd actually measure **external contacts**, not hours worked.
7. **Practice finishing deliberately.** People with high standards often accumulate beginnings. Set a definition of done *before* working: “Version 0.1 is finished when X, Y and Z happen.” Once those conditions are satisfied, declare it finished even though you can see twenty improvements. Finishing becomes its own skill.
8. **After completion, unleash your analytical ability.** This is where your tendency can become an advantage. Once something exists, ask all the difficult questions: Why didn't people use it? What's structurally wrong? What's unnecessary? How could it become exceptional? Your analytical mind becomes **critic and optimizer**, rather than gatekeeper preventing birth.
9. **Repeat complete loops, not giant projects.** Initially, I would value completing five small cycles over spending six months on one ambitious startup. The important experience is repeatedly proving: **I originated something → chose → built → encountered reality → changed it → finished.** Once that loop becomes normal, increase the size of the projects.

For the next three months, I'd therefore make your objective **not “start a successful company.”** Make it:

> **Complete 5 self-originated idea-to-reality loops.**

One might last two days. Another two weeks. One might be terrible. One might unexpectedly become interesting.

And I'd track only four numbers:

**things independently initiated → prototypes completed → external tests → projects deliberately closed**

Revenue can come later.

### Your AI analogy is actually interesting

There is a genuine parallel, although today's AI agents are more capable of autonomous action than the simple “AI only follows instructions” description suggests.

Current research still finds a large gap between performing well-defined subtasks and maintaining competent behavior across long, ambiguous trajectories. AgencyBench, for example, evaluates agents on lengthy real-world tasks and finds substantial room for improvement; other 2026 work specifically finds weaknesses in long-horizon planning and proactive assistance. [ACL Anthology+2arXiv+2](https://aclanthology.org/2026.acl-long.337/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

That's remarkably close to the distinction we're discussing:

**Competence** = “Give me the problem and I'll solve it.”

**Agency** = “Notice what needs doing, determine a goal, decide what matters, initiate action without certainty, maintain the intention over time, recognize when circumstances require adaptation, and know when to stop.”

Those are not the same capability.

And this could actually become an interesting intellectual/project direction for you.

Instead of vaguely asking **“How do we make AI more agentic?”**, you could use yourself almost as a conceptual laboratory and decompose agency into mechanisms:

> goal generation → priority selection → commitment → task decomposition → initiative → persistence → feedback → reconsideration → closure

Then ask, for each component:

**What mechanism makes humans succeed or fail here? What would the computational equivalent look like in an AI agent?**

For example, one of *your* problems is excessive reconsideration. An AI agent can have an analogous failure: continually replanning instead of executing. A possible mechanism in both cases is a **commitment window** —once a sufficiently reasonable plan is selected, alternatives are suppressed until new evidence crosses some threshold.

That is already a much more interesting research question than simply “AI agents need more agency.”

And there's a nice symmetry here: instead of waiting until you've “fixed yourself” before building something, you could potentially make **the process of learning agency itself generate prototypes and technical questions**.

That would combine the two things rather than making personal development another prerequisite you must complete before you're allowed to start.
