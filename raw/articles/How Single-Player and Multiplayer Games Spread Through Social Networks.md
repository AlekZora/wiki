# How Single-Player and Multiplayer Games Spread Through Social Networks

## Executive overview

Single-player and multiplayer games both rely heavily on social networks for discovery and growth, but they do **not** spread in the same structural way.
Multiplayer titles embed *direct network effects*—the game becomes more valuable when a player’s friends join—while single-player titles depend more on *indirect* and *asynchronous* social mechanisms such as word-of-mouth, streaming visibility, and community discourse.
Console-style and other primarily single-player games therefore lean more on a self-reinforcing “WOM snowball” process, whereas online and multiplayer games add an additional layer of compulsory recruitment and in-game social interaction that accelerates diffusion.
[^1][^2]

## Conceptual foundations: network effects vs word-of-mouth

Network effect theory distinguishes between products whose value increases directly with the number of other users (direct network effects) and products whose value increases via complementary goods, reputation, or content (indirect network effects).
Online and multiplayer games clearly fall into the direct network-effect category: more players create more opponents, teammates, matchmaking pools, and content, which in turn make the game more attractive to new entrants.
[^1]
Console and single-player games generally lack these direct in-game dependencies between users; their value proposition is primarily experienced alone, so social influence operates mainly through information (recommendations, reviews, streams) rather than through functional dependence on others’ presence.
[^2]

Word-of-mouth (WOM) marketing research shows that peer recommendations and social media feeds are now co-dominant discovery channels for games: a 2021–2024 MIDiA survey finds that roughly a quarter of consumers discover games via social feeds and another quarter via personal recommendations, ahead of ads and professional reviews.
[^3]
Agent-based models of WOM diffusion suggest that message credibility and consumer engagement can generate strong adoption cascades even in the absence of strong network effects, which is particularly relevant for single-player games whose core experience does not require friends to play.
[^4]

## Empirical evidence on single-player / console game diffusion

A 2025 BMC Psychology study on console games explicitly notes that, compared with online games, console titles “lack online social networking features” and *therefore depend more on word-of-mouth communication and experience sharing within the player community* for user growth and brand development.
[^2]
The authors model a three-stage “user snowball effect” in console gaming—acceptance, resonance, and communication—that describes how WOM builds over time.
[^2]

### Three-phase WOM snowball model for console games

The console-game WOM model identifies three key phases:

1. **Acceptance (pre-gameplay)** – Players receive and accept WOM from other users before purchase or play, forming expectations about game attractiveness and value.
2. **Resonance (during gameplay)** – Actual play generates *game attractiveness*, *pleasure arousal*, and *value perception*, which either confirm or disconfirm the earlier WOM.
3. **Communication (post-gameplay)** – When expectations are positively confirmed, players are more likely to share their experiences, producing further WOM and contributing to a snowball effect.
[^2]

Structural-equation modeling in this study shows that WOM acceptance indirectly affects later WOM communication via these mediators: game attractiveness, pleasure arousal, and value perception; additionally, *game exposure* (e.g., ads, store presence, online visibility) positively moderates the link between received WOM and both attractiveness and pleasure.
[^2]
This means that for primarily single-player/console titles, discovery often begins with a small nucleus of early adopters whose positive experiences, amplified by exposure, lead to a self-reinforcing cycle of recommendation, play, and renewed recommendation.
[^3][^2]

Crucially, in this console-focused work, other players function as *information sources* rather than as direct collaborators or opponents embedded in the game’s mechanics—the dual role of receiver and transmitter is modeled using Defleur’s two-way communication framework rather than a network-effect payoff model.
[^2]
This positions single-player diffusion as a communication-driven process: the game is *played alone but talked about together*.

## Evidence on multiplayer and online games: social interaction and direct recruitment

By contrast, research on multiplayer and social online games emphasizes the role of in-game social interaction, social identity, and prosocial behavior in driving positive WOM and continued engagement.
A 2023 study of multiplayer games finds that social interaction among players increases social identity (feeling of belonging to a gaming group) and prosocial behavior, both of which significantly predict positive WOM intentions about the game; social identity and prosocial behavior also mediate the relationship between interaction and WOM.
[^5]
This indicates that, for multiplayer games, *playing together* creates social bonds that are then expressed as recommendations and invitations.

Further work on “social online gamers” shows that many players report making friends through online play and often use games to maintain relationships with existing friends; in one survey, over half of social online gamers had met an online friend via games, and nearly three quarters had played with friends they knew from outside gaming contexts.
[^6]
During and after cooperative play, people become more prosocial in their attitudes toward teammates and even opponents, reinforcing positive social norms around the game community.
[^7][^8]
These social bonds make the invitation dynamic stronger: players recommend or “recruit” friends not merely because the game is good, but because *their social world already lives there*.

In network-effect terms, multiplayer games share characteristics with social networks like Facebook: 
if a game reaches a “critical mass” of players in a friend group or region, the increased availability of partners and community content makes the game more attractive, creating a tipping point where adoption accelerates.
[^1]
This critical mass threshold is less pronounced in single-player games because the utility of the game is not directly tied to how many of one’s friends own it; social proof matters, but functional dependence is absent.

## Discovery channels and platform differences

Cross-platform consumer surveys suggest that both single-player and multiplayer games are now discovered through a blend of offline WOM, social feeds, creator content, and advertising.
MIDiA Research reports that social media feeds and personal recommendations are tied as the leading channels for game discovery (each around one quarter of respondents), followed by social media ads and YouTube/Twitch advertising, while traditional media reviews now play a much smaller role.
[^3]
These patterns apply across genres, but multiplayer titles often gain an additional push from live-service visibility (tournaments, esports, Discord communities) that function as dense social hubs.
[^9][^1]

Console games—where single-player experiences remain dominant—must therefore convert discovery into *individual* adoption via the snowball WOM process: early recommendations shape expectations; satisfying play produces emotional arousal and perceived value; and those experiences, when shared, become the next wave of recommendations.
[^2]
In contrast, online and mobile multiplayer games can convert exposure into *group* adoption, especially when designs incorporate built-in invite mechanics, friend bonuses, or co-op requirements that reward bringing others into the game.
[^10]

## Structural comparison: how spread mechanisms differ

The table below synthesizes the main structural differences in how single-player and multiplayer games spread through social networks, based on current research.

| Dimension | Primarily Single-Player / Console | Multiplayer / Online |
|----------|------------------------------------|----------------------|
| Core network effect | Mostly **indirect** (reputation, content, reviews); game value is largely independent of friends’ adoption. | Strong **direct** network effects; value increases as friends and total playerbase grow. [^1] |
| Primary diffusion mechanism | WOM “snowball” process: acceptance → resonance → communication, driven by game attractiveness, pleasure, and value perception. [^2] | Direct recruitment via in-game social interaction, social identity, and prosocial behavior; players invite friends to play *with* them. [^5][^6] |
| Role of other players in experience | Mostly informational (recommendations, reviews, streams); others shape expectations but are not structurally required for play. [^2] | Functionally embedded (teammates, opponents, guildmates); others’ presence is part of the utility function of the game. [^1][^11] |
| Key psychological drivers of sharing | Confirmation of expectations, perceived entertainment value, emotional pleasure, perceived quality; these mediate from received WOM to outgoing WOM. [^2] | Social belonging, group identity, reciprocity, and prosocial norms formed during cooperative/competitive play; these drive invitations and positive WOM. [^5][^7] |
| Typical social graph pattern | Diffusion through broader-interest graphs (friends-of-friends, parasocial ties to streamers, general gamer circles); less dependence on dense local clusters. [^3][^2] | Diffusion through dense clusters (friend groups, clans, school cohorts) where existing ties make coordinated adoption more likely and more valuable. [^6][^12] |
| Importance of exposure / visibility | High: advertising, storefront placement, and creator coverage enhance the WOM snowball by strengthening attractiveness and pleasure links. [^2][^3] | High, but often amplified by live in-game events, esports, and community platforms that make the active playerbase visible and aspirational. [^13][^1][^9] |
| Failure mode | Game can be critically acclaimed but fail to generate enough early WOM to start the snowball; diffusion stalls despite strong individual experience. [^2][^3] | Game may reach initial user base but fail to reach network-effect tipping point; below critical mass, queues, matchmaking, and social energy collapse. [^1][^9] |

## Hybrid and boundary cases

The landscape is increasingly blurred by hybrid designs that embed “social traces” or asynchronous multiplayer into otherwise single-player experiences.
Research on social videogaming during the COVID-19 pandemic, for example, found that playing games with social features (including voice chat and online co-presence) was important for maintaining connection and well-being, even when players were not in fully synchronous competitive modes.
[^12]
This suggests that even limited social presence can make a game function more like a social platform in the player’s mental model, increasing the relevance of direct recruitment.

From a diffusion perspective, games with strong asynchronous social systems (e.g., shared infrastructure, persistent world impact, ghost data, community challenges) may benefit from *both* mechanisms: players recruit friends to “see” or contribute to their creations (a quasi-network effect), while the core loop remains viable as a solitary experience, preserving the WOM snowball of traditional single-player titles.
[^12][^2]
Such designs effectively overlay a social graph atop a single-player spine, allowing spread through co-experience, spectacle, and curiosity rather than only functional dependence.

## Synthesis: do they spread “the same way”? 

Taken together, current empirical work supports a clear distinction: single-player/console games do not rely on the same **structural** spread mechanism as multiplayer games, even though both depend on social networks.
Single-player diffusion is best described as *communication-driven WOM snowballing* in which individuals sequentially influence each other’s expectations and evaluations; multiplayer diffusion, in contrast, is *network-effect-driven recruitment* where social identity and in-game interdependence create stronger pressures to bring friends in and sustain clusters of play.
[^5][^1][^2]

In practice, this means that design and marketing levers differ: for a single-player game, the critical task is maximizing the emotional and perceived-value resonance that fuels outgoing WOM among early adopters and ensuring enough exposure for the snowball to start; for a multiplayer game, the critical task is reaching and sustaining the playerbase threshold at which playing with friends is easy and rewarding, so that invitations and social identity can do the heavy lifting.
